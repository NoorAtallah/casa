Expanding into another country can open the door to new customers, investment, partnerships and growth. But operating across borders also introduces legal and regulatory issues that may not exist in the company's home market.

A business may have a strong commercial opportunity but still face unexpected exposure through its corporate structure, contracts, licensing, tax obligations, employment arrangements, intellectual property or dispute resolution mechanisms.

The key is to identify these issues before entering the market or committing to a cross-border transaction.

What Is Cross-Border Business?

Cross-border business refers to commercial activities involving more than one country.

This can include:

  • Selling products or services internationally
  • Establishing a foreign subsidiary or branch
  • Entering international partnerships or joint ventures
  • Hiring employees in another country
  • Importing or exporting
  • Entering cross-border commercial contracts
  • Acquiring or investing in a foreign business

Each structure can create different legal obligations and risks.

1. Choose the Right Legal Structure

One of the first questions is how the business will operate in the new market.

Possible structures may include:

  • Local subsidiary
  • Branch
  • Joint venture
  • Partnership
  • Representative office
  • Contractual arrangement with a local business

The appropriate structure depends on the business model, activities, ownership, regulatory environment and long-term objectives.

The cheapest structure is not necessarily the most appropriate one.

2. Review Licensing and Regulatory Requirements

A business entering a new jurisdiction should understand what activities require:

  • Commercial licences
  • Regulatory approvals
  • Sector-specific permissions
  • Local registrations
  • Professional licences
  • Import or export approvals

Requirements can vary significantly between jurisdictions and industries.

Failing to obtain the appropriate approvals can result in penalties, restrictions on operations or contractual complications.

3. Understand the Contractual Framework

Cross-border transactions often involve multiple legal systems.

International contracts should clearly address matters such as:

  • Scope of services
  • Payment terms
  • Delivery obligations
  • Confidentiality
  • Intellectual property
  • Liability
  • Termination
  • Governing law
  • Dispute resolution

A contract that appears straightforward can create significant uncertainty if these issues are not clearly addressed.

4. Determine Which Law Governs the Contract

One of the most important provisions in a cross-border agreement is the governing law clause.

It determines which jurisdiction's law will generally be used to interpret the contract.

Businesses should consider:

  • Which law applies?
  • Why is that law appropriate?
  • Are both parties familiar with it?
  • How will it interact with mandatory local laws?

This should be considered during negotiation—not after a dispute arises.

5. Establish an Appropriate Dispute Resolution Mechanism

Cross-border disputes can become significantly more complicated when parties are located in different jurisdictions.

Contracts should consider:

  • Courts or arbitration
  • Jurisdiction
  • Arbitration seat
  • Applicable procedural rules
  • Enforcement of judgments or awards
  • Mediation requirements

The objective is to establish a clear and practical mechanism for resolving disputes before a disagreement occurs.

6. Protect Intellectual Property

International expansion can expose valuable intellectual property to new risks.

Consider:

  • Trademarks
  • Copyright
  • Patents
  • Trade secrets
  • Software
  • Brand assets
  • Proprietary information

Intellectual property protection is generally territorial, meaning protection in one country does not automatically provide equivalent protection everywhere.

Businesses expanding internationally should therefore assess where their key intellectual property needs protection.

7. Review Data Protection and Privacy Requirements

Cross-border operations may involve transferring:

  • Customer information
  • Employee data
  • Financial information
  • Commercial information

Different jurisdictions may impose different requirements relating to data collection, processing, storage and transfer.

Businesses should understand the applicable data protection and privacy obligations before transferring personal information across borders.

8. Consider Employment and Workforce Issues

Hiring or managing employees in another jurisdiction can create additional legal obligations.

These may include:

  • Employment contracts
  • Working conditions
  • Employee benefits
  • Termination requirements
  • Immigration
  • Social security
  • Local employment regulations

Using independent contractors or overseas service providers may also create legal and tax considerations.

9. Understand Tax and Financial Implications

Cross-border business can create additional tax considerations, including:

  • Corporate tax
  • Withholding tax
  • VAT or indirect taxes
  • Transfer pricing
  • Permanent establishment
  • Double-taxation issues

These matters should be considered alongside the legal and commercial structure of the transaction.

Where appropriate, businesses should obtain specialist tax advice in the relevant jurisdictions.

10. Conduct Appropriate Due Diligence

Before entering into a significant cross-border relationship, businesses should understand who they are dealing with.

Due diligence may include reviewing:

  • Corporate registration
  • Ownership
  • Directors and management
  • Financial standing
  • Litigation
  • Regulatory compliance
  • Licences
  • Contracts
  • Reputation
  • Sanctions or restricted-party considerations

The level of due diligence should reflect the size, complexity and risk of the transaction.

11. Consider Local Laws and Mandatory Requirements

Choosing a foreign governing law does not necessarily mean that all local laws can be ignored.

Certain requirements may be mandatory in the jurisdiction where the business operates.

These can relate to:

  • Licensing
  • Employment
  • Consumer protection
  • Competition
  • Data protection
  • Corporate requirements
  • Sector-specific regulation

A cross-border structure should therefore be assessed from both sides:

What does the contract say? and What does the local law require?

12. Plan for Exit and Termination

Businesses often focus heavily on entering a new market and not enough on how they will leave it.

Cross-border agreements should consider:

  • Termination rights
  • Notice periods
  • Outstanding payments
  • Intellectual property
  • Customer relationships
  • Employee obligations
  • Assets
  • Data
  • Post-termination restrictions

A clear exit strategy can prevent a difficult and expensive departure later.

The Biggest Mistake: Treating Cross-Border Business Like Domestic Business

A contract, structure or business model that works in one jurisdiction may not work in another.

The legal environment can differ significantly in areas such as:

  • Corporate law
  • Contracts
  • Employment
  • Tax
  • Data protection
  • Intellectual property
  • Licensing
  • Dispute resolution

International expansion requires more than translating an existing business model into another country. It requires understanding how that business model interacts with the new legal environment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is cross-border business?

Cross-border business involves commercial activities between two or more countries, including international sales, investments, partnerships, employment and business expansion.

What are the main legal risks of cross-border business?

Common risks include corporate structure, licensing, contracts, governing law, dispute resolution, intellectual property, data protection, employment and regulatory compliance.

Why is governing law important in an international contract?

The governing law provision determines which legal system will generally be used to interpret the contract and can significantly affect the parties' rights and obligations.

How are cross-border disputes resolved?

Depending on the contract, disputes may be resolved through courts, arbitration or mediation. The agreement should clearly establish the applicable jurisdiction or dispute resolution mechanism.

Does a business need a local company to operate internationally?

Not necessarily. The appropriate structure depends on the nature of the activities, jurisdiction, licensing requirements, ownership, tax considerations and commercial objectives.

Should businesses conduct due diligence before entering a cross-border partnership?

Yes. Appropriate due diligence can help identify ownership, financial, legal, regulatory and reputational risks before the relationship is established.

Does intellectual property protection apply internationally?

Generally, intellectual property rights are territorial. Businesses should therefore consider where their trademarks, patents, copyright and other intellectual property require protection.

Why should businesses seek legal advice before international expansion?

Cross-border expansion can involve multiple legal systems and regulatory requirements. Early legal advice can help businesses identify risks, structure transactions appropriately and avoid costly problems later.
