When a buyer is considering acquiring a business, the financial performance is only part of the picture.

Legal due diligence helps the buyer understand what they are actually acquiring, what obligations come with the business, and what risks could affect the transaction.

For sellers, understanding what buyers are likely to examine can help them prepare before the due diligence process begins.

What Do Buyers Look for During Legal Due Diligence?

While the scope varies depending on the business and transaction, buyers commonly examine ownership, contracts, corporate records, intellectual property, employees, regulatory compliance, disputes and liabilities.

The objective is to identify material issues that could affect:

  • The value of the business
  • The transaction structure
  • The purchase price
  • The buyer's future exposure
  • The terms of the sale

1. Corporate Structure and Ownership

One of the first questions is simple:

Who legally owns the business?

Buyers may review:

  • Incorporation documents
  • Shareholder records
  • Share transfers
  • Shareholder agreements
  • Corporate resolutions
  • Ownership restrictions
  • Existing investor rights

Any uncertainty regarding ownership can create significant transaction risk.

2. Commercial Contracts

Buyers will often focus on the contracts that generate or support the company's revenue.

These may include:

  • Major customer agreements
  • Supplier contracts
  • Distribution agreements
  • Strategic partnerships
  • Franchise agreements
  • Technology agreements

Particular attention may be given to change-of-control, termination, assignment, exclusivity and liability provisions.

A key contract that could disappear following an acquisition may materially affect the value of the business.

3. Intellectual Property

For many businesses, intellectual property is a significant part of enterprise value.

Buyers may want to confirm:

  • Who owns the IP
  • Whether trademarks are registered
  • Whether software rights are properly documented
  • Whether employees or contractors have assigned IP rights
  • Whether third parties have claims or restrictions over the IP

The buyer needs confidence that the business has the rights necessary to continue using its key intellectual property.

4. Litigation and Disputes

Existing or potential legal disputes can create uncertainty for a buyer.

The review may cover:

  • Current litigation
  • Threatened claims
  • Arbitration
  • Regulatory proceedings
  • Settlement agreements
  • Historical disputes with continuing obligations

The important issue is not simply whether litigation exists.

The buyer wants to understand the potential financial and commercial consequences.

5. Regulatory Compliance and Licences

Buyers will generally want to know whether the business is legally authorised to conduct its activities.

Depending on the industry, they may review:

  • Trade licences
  • Regulatory approvals
  • Permits
  • Registrations
  • Compliance records
  • Sector-specific requirements

A regulatory issue that could restrict the company's operations can become a significant transaction concern.

6. Employment and Key Personnel

Employees can represent both value and risk.

Buyers may review:

  • Employment contracts
  • Key employee arrangements
  • Senior management
  • Employee disputes
  • Benefits and obligations
  • Confidentiality provisions
  • Restrictive covenants

They may also consider how dependent the business is on particular individuals.

7. Liabilities and Obligations

Buyers need to understand what obligations may come with the business.

These may include:

  • Guarantees
  • Indemnities
  • Long-term contractual commitments
  • Financing arrangements
  • Related-party obligations
  • Potential claims

The transaction structure will determine how many of these obligations transfer or remain with the seller.

8. Assets and Ownership Rights

A buyer may also want to verify that the business actually owns or has the right to use the assets presented as part of the transaction.

This can include:

  • Equipment
  • Property
  • Intellectual property
  • Technology
  • Vehicles
  • Software
  • Digital assets

The difference between using an asset and legally owning it can become important during an acquisition.

9. Previous Agreements and Restrictions

Historical agreements can sometimes create obligations that are not immediately obvious.

Buyers may investigate:

  • Previous acquisitions
  • Investor agreements
  • Financing arrangements
  • Shareholder arrangements
  • Non-compete obligations
  • Previous settlements
  • Long-term commitments

These documents may contain restrictions affecting the proposed transaction.

10. The Company's Overall Legal Readiness

Ultimately, buyers are looking for a business where the legal position is clear, organised and understandable.

They are not necessarily looking for a company with zero legal risk.

They want to understand:

What are the risks?

How material are they?

Can they be managed?

Who should bear them?

Do they affect the value or structure of the deal?

This is why legal due diligence should be viewed as part of the commercial decision-making process, not simply a legal checklist.

What Happens When Buyers Find Legal Issues?

A legal issue does not automatically mean that a transaction will fail.

Depending on its nature, the buyer may:

  • Request further information
  • Ask the seller to resolve the issue
  • Negotiate a price adjustment
  • Request warranties or indemnities
  • Introduce conditions to completion
  • Restructure the transaction
  • Require specific protections

The earlier an issue is identified, the more options the parties generally have to address it.

How Can Sellers Prepare?

Business owners considering a sale should consider conducting a seller-side legal readiness review before approaching buyers.

This can help identify:

  • Missing documentation
  • Contractual restrictions
  • Ownership issues
  • IP gaps
  • Compliance concerns
  • Potential liabilities
  • Existing disputes

The objective is simple:

Know what the buyer is likely to find before the buyer starts looking.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do buyers check during legal due diligence?

Buyers typically review ownership, corporate records, contracts, intellectual property, employees, licences, regulatory compliance, litigation, liabilities and material obligations.

Why do buyers conduct legal due diligence?

To understand the legal position of the business and identify risks that could affect value, transaction terms, future liabilities or the decision to proceed.

Can legal due diligence reduce the purchase price?

Yes. Material legal risks may lead a buyer to negotiate a price adjustment, additional protections or changes to the transaction structure.

Do buyers expect a business to have no legal risks?

No. Every business has some level of risk. Buyers generally want to understand the nature, significance and manageability of those risks.

Should a seller conduct legal due diligence?

A seller-side legal review can help identify and address potential issues before the buyer's due diligence begins, potentially making the transaction more efficient.

How can a business prepare for buyer due diligence?

A business can prepare by organising its corporate records, reviewing contracts and IP ownership, checking licences and compliance, identifying disputes and liabilities, and addressing material legal gaps.
