A strong relationship between business owners is valuable—but it should not rely entirely on trust or informal understandings.

As a company grows, differences can arise over decision-making, funding, dividends, management, ownership and the future direction of the business.

A well-drafted shareholder agreement can provide a clear framework for dealing with these issues before they become disputes.

For business owners, the objective is not to anticipate every possible disagreement. It is to establish clear rules for the situations that could materially affect the company or its shareholders.

What Is a Shareholder Agreement?

A shareholder agreement is a private agreement between some or all shareholders that sets out their rights, responsibilities and arrangements regarding the ownership and management of a company.

It can complement the company's constitutional documents and corporate governance framework.

The appropriate provisions depend on the company's structure, jurisdiction, number of shareholders and commercial objectives.

1. Ownership and Shareholding

The agreement should clearly establish:

  • Each shareholder's ownership percentage
  • Share classes and associated rights
  • Voting rights
  • Existing and agreed future ownership arrangements

Clear documentation can help prevent disagreements about ownership and control.

2. Decision-Making and Voting Rights

Not every business decision should be treated in the same way.

A shareholder agreement can establish which matters:

  • Management can decide
  • Require board approval
  • Require shareholder approval
  • Require unanimous consent

This can be particularly important where there are minority shareholders or multiple owners.

3. Reserved Matters

Certain decisions may have a significant impact on the company and therefore require enhanced approval.

Examples may include:

  • Issuing new shares
  • Selling significant assets
  • Taking substantial borrowing
  • Acquiring another business
  • Entering major transactions
  • Changing the company's business
  • Distributing significant dividends

The agreement should clearly identify the approval threshold for these matters.

4. Roles and Responsibilities

Where shareholders are also involved in management, the agreement can help distinguish ownership rights from management responsibilities.

It may address:

  • Management roles
  • Board appointments
  • Authority levels
  • Reporting responsibilities
  • Remuneration
  • Founder responsibilities

This can reduce confusion between being a shareholder and being an executive.

5. Funding and Additional Capital

A growing company may require additional funding.

Shareholders should consider what happens if the company needs:

  • Additional equity
  • Shareholder loans
  • External financing
  • Emergency funding

The agreement can establish whether shareholders are expected to contribute and what happens if one shareholder cannot or does not wish to participate.

6. Transfer of Shares

Shareholders may eventually want to sell or transfer their shares.

The agreement should consider:

  • Who can transfer shares
  • Restrictions on transfers
  • Transfers to existing shareholders
  • Transfers to third parties
  • Approval requirements
  • Valuation mechanisms

Clear transfer provisions can help protect the existing ownership structure.

7. Pre-Emption Rights

Pre-emption provisions can give existing shareholders the opportunity to purchase shares before they are offered to an outside party.

This can help prevent an unwanted third party from becoming a shareholder.

The agreement should clearly establish how the process works and within what timeframe.

8. Exit and Sale Provisions

Shareholders may have different views about when or how to exit the business.

A shareholder agreement can address:

  • Voluntary exits
  • Sale of the company
  • Share transfers
  • Drag-along rights
  • Tag-along rights
  • Valuation
  • Exit procedures

These provisions can become particularly important when the company receives an acquisition offer.

9. Deadlock

A deadlock can occur when shareholders cannot agree on an important decision.

A well-structured agreement can establish a process for resolving deadlock, potentially involving:

Negotiation → Mediation → Arbitration or other agreed mechanism

The appropriate mechanism depends on the circumstances and applicable law.

10. Dividends and Profit Distribution

Shareholders may have different expectations regarding the use of company profits.

The agreement can establish principles around:

  • Dividend distributions
  • Retained earnings
  • Reinvestment
  • Shareholder expectations
  • Approval requirements

This can help align shareholder expectations with the company's growth strategy.

11. Confidentiality and Restrictive Obligations

Shareholders may have access to commercially sensitive information.

The agreement may therefore include provisions relating to:

  • Confidentiality
  • Non-solicitation
  • Non-compete obligations, where legally enforceable
  • Protection of intellectual property
  • Use of company information

These provisions should be drafted carefully to reflect applicable law.

12. Death, Incapacity or Other Unexpected Events

Shareholder arrangements should also consider what happens if a shareholder:

  • Dies
  • Becomes incapacitated
  • Becomes insolvent
  • Loses a required licence
  • Leaves the business

Appropriate provisions can help protect both the company and the remaining shareholders during unexpected circumstances.

Why Shareholder Agreements Matter

A shareholder agreement can provide clarity on issues that may otherwise become sources of conflict.

It can help answer questions such as:

Who makes the decision?

What happens if shareholders disagree?

Can a shareholder sell their shares to someone outside the company?

What happens if the business needs more funding?

What happens if one shareholder wants to leave?

What happens if the company receives an offer to be acquired?

Having these principles agreed in advance can make difficult situations easier to manage.

When Should Business Owners Consider a Shareholder Agreement?

A shareholder agreement can be particularly valuable:

  • When establishing a company with multiple shareholders
  • When bringing in a new investor
  • When ownership changes
  • When founders have different roles
  • Before significant external investment
  • Before a major expansion
  • When a shareholder wants to exit
  • When preparing for a future sale

It is generally easier to agree on the rules before a disagreement exists.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the purpose of a shareholder agreement?

A shareholder agreement establishes the rights, responsibilities and arrangements between shareholders, particularly regarding ownership, decision-making, transfers, funding and exits.

Is a shareholder agreement legally required?

This depends on the applicable jurisdiction and company structure. Even where it is not mandatory, a shareholder agreement can provide important protection and clarity between business owners.

What should a shareholder agreement include?

Key provisions may cover ownership, voting rights, reserved matters, management roles, funding, share transfers, pre-emption, exits, deadlock, dividends and unexpected events.

Why are deadlock provisions important?

Without an agreed mechanism, a serious disagreement between shareholders can prevent the company from making important decisions. A deadlock provision establishes a process for attempting to resolve the disagreement.

What are tag-along and drag-along rights?

Tag-along rights can protect minority shareholders when a majority shareholder sells their shares. Drag-along rights can allow majority shareholders to require minority shareholders to participate in a qualifying sale, subject to the applicable legal and contractual framework.

Should an existing shareholder agreement be reviewed?

Yes. A shareholder agreement should be reviewed when there are significant changes to ownership, investment, management, business activities or the company's strategic direction.
