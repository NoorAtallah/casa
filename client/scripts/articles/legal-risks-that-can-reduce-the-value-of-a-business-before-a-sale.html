A business may have strong revenue, healthy margins and attractive growth prospects, yet still face legal issues that reduce what a buyer is willing to pay.

During an acquisition, buyers are not only evaluating what the business earns today. They are also assessing the risks that could affect its future performance and ownership.

Some legal risks can be managed during negotiations. Others can directly affect valuation, transaction structure or the buyer's willingness to proceed.

For business owners considering a sale, identifying these risks early can help protect the value they have built.

What Legal Risks Can Reduce Business Value?

The most significant risks are usually those that create uncertainty around ownership, revenue, liabilities, compliance, contracts or the ability to operate the business after the transaction.

Here are some of the key areas sellers should consider.

1. Unclear Ownership or Corporate Structure

A buyer needs confidence that the seller has the legal right to sell the business.

Problems can arise from:

  • Unclear share ownership
  • Incomplete corporate records
  • Undocumented share transfers
  • Disputes between shareholders
  • Unresolved ownership rights
  • Restrictions on transferring shares

Even if the underlying business is strong, uncertainty around ownership can delay or complicate a transaction.

2. Weak or Unfavourable Commercial Contracts

Contracts are often closely connected to the value of a business.

A buyer may become concerned where important contracts contain:

  • Short termination rights
  • Change-of-control provisions
  • Assignment restrictions
  • Unfavourable liability clauses
  • Unclear renewal arrangements
  • Excessive exclusivity obligations

If a significant customer can terminate its contract following an acquisition, for example, the buyer may reassess the value of the business.

3. Dependence on Undocumented Business Relationships

Some businesses rely heavily on relationships that are not adequately documented.

For example:

  • Major customers operate without formal agreements
  • Important suppliers have informal arrangements
  • Key partnerships are based primarily on personal relationships
  • Important commercial terms are not clearly recorded

This can create uncertainty about whether those relationships will survive a change in ownership.

4. Intellectual Property Ownership Issues

A business may rely heavily on intellectual property without having clear evidence of ownership.

Potential concerns include:

  • Unregistered trademarks
  • Software developed by external contractors
  • Missing IP assignment agreements
  • Third-party licensing restrictions
  • Unclear ownership of digital assets

If a valuable business asset cannot be clearly linked to the company, a buyer may question its true value.

5. Existing or Potential Litigation

Legal disputes can create uncertainty about future liabilities.

Buyers may examine:

  • Current litigation
  • Threatened claims
  • Arbitration
  • Regulatory proceedings
  • Historical disputes
  • Settlement obligations

The impact will depend on the nature and materiality of the issue.

However, undisclosed or poorly managed disputes can significantly undermine buyer confidence.

6. Regulatory or Licensing Problems

A business must be able to legally continue its operations after the transaction.

Potential concerns include:

  • Expired licences
  • Missing approvals
  • Regulatory breaches
  • Non-compliance with industry requirements
  • Restrictions on ownership or activities

For regulated businesses, these issues can become particularly significant.

7. Employment and Key-Person Risks

Employees can be an important part of business value.

Buyers may become concerned where:

  • Key employees have no formal contracts
  • Important staff are likely to leave after the sale
  • Employee disputes exist
  • Confidentiality protections are weak
  • The business is highly dependent on the owner

A business that cannot operate effectively without its founder may be viewed as carrying additional transition risk.

8. Undisclosed Liabilities

A buyer needs to understand what obligations may accompany the acquisition.

These can include:

  • Guarantees
  • Indemnities
  • Contractual commitments
  • Related-party arrangements
  • Financing obligations
  • Potential claims

Unexpected liabilities can affect both valuation and transaction terms.

9. Poor Corporate and Legal Documentation

Sometimes the problem is not a major legal dispute—it is simply poor documentation.

Examples include:

  • Missing contracts
  • Incomplete corporate records
  • Outdated agreements
  • Inconsistent company information
  • Missing board or shareholder resolutions

These issues can create unnecessary questions during due diligence and make the business appear less prepared than it actually is.

10. Excessive Dependence on the Founder

This is both a commercial and legal consideration.

If the founder personally controls:

  • Key customer relationships
  • Important contracts
  • Business know-how
  • Supplier relationships
  • Operational decisions

the buyer may question how transferable the business really is.

Reducing unnecessary founder dependency before a sale can help create a more sustainable and transferable business.

Can Legal Risks Actually Reduce the Sale Price?

Yes.

The effect depends on the nature and materiality of the risk.

A buyer may respond by:

  • Reducing the purchase price
  • Requesting additional warranties
  • Seeking specific indemnities
  • Requiring the seller to resolve the issue
  • Retaining part of the purchase price
  • Changing the transaction structure
  • Introducing additional conditions to completion

In some cases, a significant unresolved issue may cause the buyer to reconsider the transaction altogether.

Not Every Legal Risk Is a Deal Breaker

The goal of preparing a business for sale is not to eliminate every possible legal risk.

Instead, business owners should understand:

What is the risk?

How significant is it?

Can it be resolved?

Can it be managed contractually?

Could it affect the value of the business?

This allows the seller to address the right issues rather than trying to achieve an unrealistic standard of zero risk.

How Can Business Owners Protect Value Before a Sale?

A seller-side legal readiness review can help identify issues before entering negotiations.

Depending on the findings, the business may be able to:

  • Formalise important contracts
  • Clarify ownership
  • Resolve outstanding disputes
  • Update corporate records
  • Strengthen IP documentation
  • Address compliance gaps
  • Reduce contractual exposure
  • Improve management continuity

The principle is simple:

If a buyer is likely to discover it, consider addressing it before the buyer does.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can legal problems reduce the value of a business?

Yes. Material legal risks can affect buyer confidence, valuation, transaction structure and the terms of the sale.

What legal issues do buyers look for when buying a business?

Common areas include ownership, contracts, intellectual property, litigation, regulatory compliance, employees, liabilities and corporate documentation.

Should I fix legal issues before selling my business?

Where practical, material issues should be identified and addressed before approaching buyers. Other risks may be managed through appropriate transaction terms and protections.

Can contracts affect business valuation?

Yes. Important contracts with termination, assignment or change-of-control restrictions can affect the sustainability of revenue and therefore the perceived value of the business.

Does litigation always reduce the value of a business?

Not necessarily. The impact depends on the nature, materiality, potential liability and likely outcome of the dispute.

How can I identify legal risks before selling my company?

A seller-side legal readiness or pre-sale legal review can help identify material issues before the buyer conducts its own due diligence.
