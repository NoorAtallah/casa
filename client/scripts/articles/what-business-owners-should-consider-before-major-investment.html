Major investments can accelerate growth, strengthen a company's market position and create new revenue opportunities. But they can also tie up significant capital and expose a business to financial, operational and strategic risk.

Whether the investment involves new equipment, property, technology, expansion, an acquisition or a new business venture, the decision should be based on more than an attractive opportunity.

Before committing significant capital, business owners should ask a fundamental question:

Does the expected value justify the investment, risk and resources required?

What Should a Business Consider Before Making a Major Investment?

A sound investment decision should consider more than the purchase price. Business owners should assess the commercial opportunity, financial return, cash-flow impact, risks and strategic fit.

Here are the key considerations.

1. Strategic Fit

An investment should support the company's broader objectives.

Ask:

  • Does it support our growth strategy?
  • Does it strengthen our competitive position?
  • Does it address a genuine business need?
  • Will it create a new capability or opportunity?
  • Does it fit our long-term objectives?

An investment can be profitable in isolation but still be the wrong decision if it distracts the business from its core strategy.

2. The Expected Return

Before investing, determine what the business expects to receive in return.

Depending on the investment, this could include:

  • Additional revenue
  • Increased profitability
  • Cost savings
  • Improved efficiency
  • New customers
  • Market access
  • Increased business value

The expected return should be supported by realistic assumptions rather than optimistic projections.

3. Total Investment Cost

The purchase price is rarely the full cost of an investment.

Consider:

  • Acquisition cost
  • Implementation costs
  • Professional fees
  • Training
  • Maintenance
  • Financing costs
  • Additional employees
  • Technology
  • Marketing
  • Working capital

Understanding the total cost of ownership can materially change the investment decision.

4. Cash-Flow Impact

A business may be profitable and still be unable to comfortably fund a major investment.

Consider:

  • How much cash is required upfront?
  • When will additional revenue be generated?
  • When will the investment reach break-even?
  • Will working capital requirements increase?
  • What happens to liquidity if revenue takes longer to materialise?

Cash-flow planning is particularly important where the investment requires substantial upfront expenditure.

5. Funding Structure

How the investment is funded can be as important as the investment itself.

Potential funding sources may include:

  • Existing cash
  • Bank financing
  • Investor capital
  • Shareholder funding
  • Leasing
  • Strategic partners

Each option has different implications for cost, ownership, cash flow, control and financial risk.

6. Test Different Scenarios

Do not rely on one financial projection.

At a minimum, consider:

Base Case

The expected outcome based on reasonable assumptions.

Downside Case

Revenue is lower, costs are higher or implementation takes longer.

Upside Case

The investment performs better than expected.

Scenario analysis helps answer an important question:

Can the business absorb the investment if the expected outcome does not materialise?

7. Assess the Risks

Every major investment carries risk.

Consider risks relating to:

  • Market demand
  • Competition
  • Technology
  • Regulation
  • Suppliers
  • Customers
  • Financing
  • Implementation
  • Management capacity

The objective is not to eliminate risk, but to understand which risks the business is accepting and how they can be managed.

8. Consider the Opportunity Cost

Capital used for one investment cannot normally be used simultaneously for another.

Ask:

What else could we do with this capital?

For example, the business could potentially:

  • Expand another operation
  • Hire key employees
  • Reduce debt
  • Invest in technology
  • Enter a different market
  • Strengthen cash reserves

The investment should therefore be evaluated against realistic alternatives, not simply on its own merits.

9. Evaluate Operational Readiness

An investment may require significant changes to how the business operates.

Consider whether the company has:

  • Appropriate employees
  • Management capacity
  • Systems
  • Technology
  • Infrastructure
  • Processes
  • Suppliers

Buying the right asset or entering the right market does not guarantee success if the business cannot effectively implement and operate the investment.

10. Consider the Impact on Business Value

A major investment should ideally contribute to long-term enterprise value.

Consider whether it will:

  • Increase sustainable earnings
  • Strengthen recurring revenue
  • Improve margins
  • Reduce operational risk
  • Create competitive advantages
  • Increase scalability
  • Improve the company's attractiveness to future investors or buyers

Not every investment that increases revenue necessarily increases business value.

11. Conduct Appropriate Due Diligence

The level of due diligence should reflect the size and complexity of the investment.

Depending on the transaction, this may involve:

  • Financial due diligence
  • Commercial due diligence
  • Legal due diligence
  • Tax review
  • Operational assessment
  • Technical review
  • Market analysis

For an acquisition or strategic investment, proper due diligence can identify issues that may materially affect the expected return.

12. Define Your Exit or Contingency Plan

Before investing, consider what happens if the investment does not perform as expected.

Ask:

  • Can the investment be sold?
  • Can the project be scaled back?
  • What is the maximum acceptable loss?
  • When should management reassess the investment?
  • What conditions would justify stopping further investment?

A contingency plan does not indicate a lack of confidence.

It demonstrates disciplined investment decision-making.

A Practical Investment Decision Framework

Before committing significant capital, business owners should be able to answer:

Common Investment Mistakes

Focusing on the opportunity instead of the economics

A compelling opportunity still needs to generate sufficient value relative to its cost and risk.

Underestimating the total investment

Implementation, staffing, financing and working-capital requirements can significantly increase the true cost.

Using overly optimistic assumptions

Financial projections should be tested against realistic and downside scenarios.

Ignoring cash flow

An investment can be profitable over time while creating immediate liquidity pressure.

Overlooking opportunity cost

Capital should be compared against other realistic uses.

Failing to define success

Before investing, management should establish what success looks like and how it will be measured.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a business consider before making a major investment?

Key considerations include strategic fit, expected return, total investment cost, cash-flow impact, funding, risks, operational readiness, opportunity cost and potential impact on business value.

How do you evaluate whether an investment is worthwhile?

A business should compare the expected financial and strategic benefits against the total cost, risks, cash-flow requirements and alternative uses of capital.

Why is cash flow important when making an investment?

Major investments often require substantial expenditure before additional revenue or savings are realised. Cash-flow analysis helps determine whether the business can fund the investment without creating excessive liquidity pressure.

Should a business prepare financial projections before investing?

Yes. Financial projections can help estimate investment requirements, revenue, costs, profitability, cash flow, break-even and potential return. The assumptions should also be tested through scenario analysis.

What is opportunity cost in investment decisions?

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative use of the capital or resources being committed to the investment.

When should a business conduct due diligence?

Due diligence is particularly important when an investment involves another company, significant capital, intellectual property, contracts, regulatory exposure or other material risks.

Should every major investment have an exit plan?

An appropriate contingency or exit strategy can help management determine when to reassess, reduce exposure or stop investing if the original assumptions no longer hold.
