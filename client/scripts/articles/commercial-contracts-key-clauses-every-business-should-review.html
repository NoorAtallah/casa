A commercial contract does more than document an agreement. It defines the rights, obligations, risks and responsibilities of the parties—and can have a significant impact on a business long after it is signed.

Whether you are dealing with a customer, supplier, partner, consultant or investor, the most important question is not simply "What are we agreeing to?" but also "What risks are we accepting?"

A well-structured contract should reflect the commercial agreement while protecting the business against foreseeable risks.

What Should Businesses Look for in a Commercial Contract?

While every contract should be reviewed according to its specific circumstances, several clauses deserve particular attention.

1. Scope of Services and Obligations

The contract should clearly establish what each party is expected to deliver.

Unclear responsibilities, deliverables or timelines can create different interpretations of the agreement and eventually lead to disputes.

Key consideration: Does the contract accurately reflect what was commercially agreed?

2. Price and Payment Terms

Payment provisions should clearly address the agreed price, payment schedule, invoicing requirements, deadlines and applicable taxes.

Businesses should also consider whether the payment structure is consistent with their cash-flow requirements and commercial objectives.

A profitable transaction can still create financial pressure if payment terms are poorly structured.

3. Term, Renewal and Termination

Businesses should understand when the agreement begins, when it ends, whether it automatically renews, and under what circumstances either party can terminate it.

Particular attention should be given to:

  • Notice periods
  • Termination for breach
  • Termination for convenience
  • Consequences of termination
  • Automatic renewal provisions

A contract that is easy to enter into may not necessarily be easy to exit.

4. Liability and Indemnities

Liability and indemnity clauses determine how financial responsibility is allocated if something goes wrong.

Businesses should consider whether liability is capped, what types of losses are excluded, and whether indemnification obligations could create significant exposure.

This is often one of the most commercially important areas of a contract—and one of the easiest to overlook.

5. Confidentiality and Intellectual Property

Contracts often involve the exchange of sensitive business information or the creation of intellectual property.

The agreement should make clear:

  • What information is confidential
  • How it may be used
  • How long confidentiality obligations continue
  • Who owns existing and newly created intellectual property
  • What happens to that information or IP when the relationship ends

This is particularly important for businesses working with consultants, technology providers, agencies and strategic partners.

6. Governing Law and Dispute Resolution

A contract should clearly establish which law applies and how disputes will be resolved.

Depending on the transaction, this may involve courts, arbitration, mediation or another agreed mechanism.

For cross-border contracts, these provisions become particularly important because the parties may operate under different legal systems.

7. Assignment and Change of Control

Businesses should also consider whether the contract can be transferred to another party and what happens if the ownership or control of a company changes.

This can become particularly important when a business is:

  • Being sold
  • Acquiring another company
  • Restructuring
  • Entering into a joint venture
  • Transferring a contract within a corporate group

A contract that restricts assignment or contains change-of-control provisions can potentially affect a future transaction.

What Business Owners Often Miss

Many businesses focus heavily on the price and commercial terms while paying less attention to the clauses that determine what happens when circumstances change.

For example, a contract may appear commercially attractive but contain:

  • Broad indemnities
  • Unlimited liability
  • Automatic renewal
  • Difficult termination conditions
  • Unclear deliverables
  • Restrictive intellectual property provisions
  • Unfavourable dispute-resolution mechanisms

These provisions may have little impact when everything goes well—but can become critical when the relationship breaks down.

The real value of contract review is therefore not simply identifying what the contract says. It is understanding what those provisions could mean for your business.

When Should a Business Have a Contract Professionally Reviewed?

Not every routine agreement requires the same level of scrutiny. However, professional legal review becomes particularly valuable when a contract:

  • Involves significant financial commitments
  • Creates long-term obligations
  • Contains substantial liability or indemnity provisions
  • Involves intellectual property or confidential information
  • Relates to an investment, acquisition or partnership
  • Involves parties in different jurisdictions
  • Could materially affect the business if the relationship ends

The cost of reviewing an important contract before signing can be significantly lower than the cost of dealing with an avoidable dispute later.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a commercial contract?

A commercial contract is a legally binding agreement governing a business relationship or transaction between two or more parties.

What clauses should I review before signing a business contract?

Key areas include scope and obligations, payment terms, termination, liability and indemnities, confidentiality, intellectual property, dispute resolution, governing law and assignment.

Why is commercial contract review important?

A contract review can help identify unclear obligations, unfavorable terms and potential legal or commercial risks before they become disputes or financial problems.

Should every contract be reviewed by a lawyer?

Not necessarily. The level of review should reflect the value, complexity and risk of the agreement. Material or complex commercial contracts generally warrant professional legal review.
