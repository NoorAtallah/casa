A contract can look perfectly reasonable and still expose a business to significant legal, financial, or operational risk.

For business owners, the issue is rarely whether a contract contains legal language. The real question is whether the terms protect the business and reflect the commercial deal that was actually intended.

A poorly structured agreement may create problems with cash flow, liability, termination, intellectual property, or disputes—often when the business is least prepared to deal with them.

What Are the Most Common Contract Risks for Businesses?

1. Unclear Scope and Obligations

One of the most common contract risks is simply lack of clarity.

If the contract does not clearly define what each party must deliver, disagreements can arise over:

  • Scope of work
  • Deliverables
  • Responsibilities
  • Deadlines
  • Performance standards

The more ambiguous the obligation, the greater the potential for disagreement.

A good contract should leave as little room as possible for different interpretations of the commercial deal.

2. Unfavourable Payment Terms

Payment provisions can create financial pressure even when the underlying transaction is profitable.

Businesses should consider whether the contract contains:

  • Long payment periods
  • Unclear invoicing requirements
  • Significant upfront commitments
  • Unfavourable payment milestones
  • Unclear provisions for disputed invoices
  • Additional or unexpected charges

A contract should be assessed not only for its total value but also for its impact on cash flow and working capital.

3. Excessive Liability

Liability provisions can expose a business to losses that are disproportionate to the value of the contract.

Pay particular attention to:

  • Unlimited liability
  • High liability caps
  • Broad definitions of loss
  • Exceptions to liability limitations
  • Third-party claims

A business should understand the maximum financial exposure it could potentially face if the relationship goes wrong.

4. Broad Indemnity Obligations

An indemnity may require one party to compensate the other for specified losses, claims, or liabilities.

The risk depends heavily on the wording.

A broad indemnity could create obligations that extend well beyond the original commercial value of the agreement.

Never treat an indemnity as standard wording without understanding exactly what it covers.

5. Difficult or One-Sided Termination Rights

A contract should provide a clear and commercially workable exit mechanism.

Risk can arise where:

  • One party can terminate easily while the other cannot
  • Notice periods are excessively long
  • Termination penalties are significant
  • There is automatic renewal
  • Termination consequences are unclear

Business owners should ask:

If this relationship no longer works for my business, how difficult—and expensive—will it be to exit?

6. Automatic Renewal

Automatic renewal clauses are easy to overlook.

A business may assume that a contract simply expires, only to discover that it has renewed because the required notice was not provided within a specific period.

Important dates should be monitored internally, particularly for significant supplier, service, lease, technology, and subscription agreements.

7. Intellectual Property Ownership

Businesses can unintentionally give away valuable intellectual property if ownership and usage rights are not clearly addressed.

This is particularly relevant for:

  • Software
  • Designs
  • Branding
  • Content
  • Consulting deliverables
  • Research
  • Technology

The contract should make clear what each party owns before the engagement and what happens to newly created work.

8. Confidentiality and Information Risks

Businesses regularly share commercially sensitive information with third parties.

Weak confidentiality provisions can create exposure around:

  • Customer information
  • Pricing
  • Financial data
  • Business strategies
  • Trade secrets
  • Technical information

Businesses should also consider how long confidentiality obligations continue after the contract ends.

9. Unfavourable Dispute Resolution

A contract may be commercially attractive until a dispute arises.

The dispute-resolution clause can determine:

  • Where proceedings take place
  • Which law applies
  • Whether disputes go to court or arbitration
  • Whether mediation is required
  • The process for escalating disputes

This becomes particularly important in cross-border commercial agreements.

A dispute-resolution mechanism that is difficult or expensive to use can significantly increase the cost of enforcing your rights.

10. Restrictions on Assignment or Change of Control

A contract may restrict a company from transferring its rights or obligations without the other party's consent.

This can become a significant issue if the business later:

  • Is sold
  • Takes on an investor
  • Restructures
  • Enters into a joint venture
  • Transfers activities to another group company

A contract that creates restrictions on a future transaction can potentially affect the value and flexibility of the business.

The Risk of Using "Standard" Contracts

One of the most common mistakes businesses make is assuming that a standard contract is automatically a safe contract.

Templates can be useful starting points, but every business and transaction is different.

A contract designed for one type of relationship may not be appropriate for another.

The important question is not:

"Is this a standard clause?"

It is:

"Is this clause appropriate for our business, this transaction and the risks we are prepared to accept?"

Not Every Risk Needs to Be Eliminated

Effective contract review is not about trying to remove every possible risk.

Business involves risk.

The objective is to identify, understand, allocate and manage that risk.

For example, a business may accept a particular liability exposure because the commercial opportunity justifies it. The important point is that the decision should be made consciously—not because the provision was overlooked.

This is where legal advice can add real value.

When Should You Have a Contract Professionally Reviewed?

Professional contract review is particularly important where an agreement:

  • Involves substantial financial commitments
  • Creates long-term obligations
  • Contains significant liability or indemnities
  • Involves intellectual property
  • Relates to an acquisition, investment or partnership
  • Involves different jurisdictions
  • Contains complex termination provisions
  • Could materially affect the business if the relationship fails

The objective is not simply to find problems. It is to determine which risks matter, which can be negotiated, and which the business can reasonably accept.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the most common contract risks for businesses?

Common risks include unclear obligations, unfavourable payment terms, excessive liability, broad indemnities, difficult termination provisions, automatic renewal, IP ownership issues, confidentiality risks, and unfavourable dispute-resolution provisions.

Can a contract expose my business to more risk than its value?

Yes. Liability, indemnity, warranty, and other provisions can potentially create financial exposure that is significantly greater than the contract's headline value.

Are standard contract templates safe for businesses?

Not necessarily. A template may provide a useful starting point, but its terms should be assessed against the specific transaction, business model, applicable law and risk profile.

Should I negotiate every contract clause?

Not necessarily. The objective is not to negotiate everything. It is to identify the provisions that materially affect your legal position, financial exposure and commercial objectives.

When should I seek professional contract advice?

Consider professional review when the agreement involves significant financial commitments, long-term obligations, substantial liability, intellectual property, cross-border transactions, investment, acquisitions or other material business risks.
