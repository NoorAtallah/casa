Doing business across borders creates opportunities—but it also introduces legal and commercial risks that may not arise in a domestic transaction.

A cross-border commercial contract may involve different legal systems, currencies, jurisdictions, regulatory requirements and enforcement mechanisms. A clause that appears straightforward in one country can have very different consequences when the other party is based elsewhere.

For businesses entering international agreements, the objective should not simply be to sign a contract. It should be to understand the risks, allocate responsibilities clearly and protect the business if circumstances change.

What Is a Cross-Border Commercial Contract?

A cross-border commercial contract is an agreement between parties operating in different countries or jurisdictions.

Examples include:

  • International supply agreements
  • Distribution agreements
  • Service agreements
  • Agency agreements
  • Joint venture arrangements
  • Technology and licensing agreements
  • Consulting agreements
  • International sales agreements

The legal and commercial framework should be considered carefully before the agreement is signed.

Key Risks Businesses Should Consider

1. Governing Law

One of the most important questions is:

Which country's law will govern the contract?

The governing law determines how contractual provisions may be interpreted and enforced.

Businesses should not assume that their home country's law will automatically apply.

The choice of governing law should be considered based on the transaction, jurisdictions involved and overall risk profile.

2. Jurisdiction and Dispute Resolution

A contract should clearly address where and how disputes will be resolved.

Possible mechanisms include:

  • Local courts
  • Foreign courts
  • Arbitration
  • Mediation
  • Multi-step dispute resolution procedures

For cross-border transactions, the practical ability to enforce a judgment or arbitral award can be just as important as the dispute mechanism itself.

3. Contract Enforcement

Having a signed contract does not necessarily mean that enforcing it will be straightforward.

Businesses should consider:

  • Where the counterparty's assets are located
  • Whether judgments are recognised in the relevant jurisdiction
  • Whether arbitration awards can be enforced
  • The cost and duration of enforcement
  • Whether local proceedings may be required

These considerations should be assessed before the dispute arises.

4. Currency and Payment Risk

International contracts often involve different currencies.

Consider:

  • Contract currency
  • Exchange-rate fluctuations
  • Payment deadlines
  • Bank charges
  • Transfer restrictions
  • Late-payment consequences
  • Currency conversion responsibilities

Where a transaction is long-term or high-value, currency risk can materially affect commercial returns.

5. Tax and Withholding Obligations

Cross-border payments may trigger tax obligations in one or more jurisdictions.

Depending on the transaction, businesses may need to consider:

  • Withholding tax
  • VAT or indirect taxes
  • Corporate tax
  • Transfer pricing
  • Permanent establishment risks
  • Double-taxation considerations

The contract should clearly allocate responsibility for applicable taxes and related costs.

6. Regulatory and Compliance Risk

A transaction that is commercially acceptable may still be subject to regulatory restrictions.

Depending on the countries and industries involved, businesses may need to consider:

  • Licensing
  • Import and export controls
  • Sanctions
  • Anti-bribery requirements
  • Anti-money laundering rules
  • Data protection
  • Industry-specific regulations

Compliance should be assessed based on both jurisdictions, not only the country where your company is established.

7. Liability and Indemnities

Cross-border contracts should clearly establish who is responsible when something goes wrong.

Important provisions may include:

  • Limitation of liability
  • Indemnities
  • Exclusions of liability
  • Direct and indirect losses
  • Caps on liability
  • Insurance requirements

Poorly structured liability provisions can expose a business to significant financial risk.

8. Intellectual Property

International contracts should clearly establish ownership and permitted use of intellectual property.

This may include:

  • Trademarks
  • Copyright
  • Software
  • Designs
  • Technical information
  • Confidential know-how
  • Business methodologies

The contract should clarify who owns the intellectual property, who can use it and what happens when the relationship ends.

9. Confidentiality and Data Protection

Cross-border transactions often involve sharing commercially sensitive information.

Businesses should consider:

  • Confidentiality obligations
  • Data access
  • Data storage
  • Cross-border data transfers
  • Cybersecurity responsibilities
  • Data breach obligations

Where personal data is involved, applicable data protection laws should be assessed separately.

10. Delivery, Performance and Force Majeure

International transactions may be affected by events outside either party's control.

Contracts should consider events such as:

  • Government restrictions
  • Trade restrictions
  • War or civil unrest
  • Natural disasters
  • Supply chain disruption
  • Transportation interruptions
  • Regulatory changes

A carefully drafted force majeure clause can help establish what happens when contractual performance becomes impossible or significantly disrupted.

11. Termination Rights

Businesses should understand when and how they can exit the relationship.

Review:

  • Termination for convenience
  • Termination for breach
  • Notice periods
  • Insolvency
  • Regulatory changes
  • Change of control
  • Post-termination obligations

A contract that is difficult to exit can create significant commercial exposure.

12. Language and Interpretation

Where parties operate in different languages, the contract should clearly identify the authoritative version.

If multiple language versions exist, specify which version prevails in the event of inconsistency.

This can help avoid disputes caused by differences in translation or interpretation.

The Biggest Mistake: Treating an International Contract Like a Domestic Contract

One of the most common mistakes businesses make is using an existing domestic contract with only minor changes for an international transaction.

Cross-border contracts require consideration of additional legal, regulatory, financial and enforcement issues.

The contract should reflect the actual jurisdictions, transaction structure and commercial relationship involved.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main risks in cross-border commercial contracts?

The main risks include governing law, dispute resolution, enforcement, currency, taxation, regulatory compliance, liability, intellectual property, confidentiality and termination.

Why is governing law important in an international contract?

Governing law determines the legal framework used to interpret the agreement and can significantly influence the parties' rights and obligations.

Should international contracts include an arbitration clause?

Arbitration can be appropriate for certain cross-border transactions, but whether it is suitable depends on the jurisdictions, transaction, parties and enforcement considerations.

What should businesses consider when choosing jurisdiction?

Businesses should consider where disputes can be resolved efficiently and whether a court judgment or arbitral award is likely to be recognised and enforceable where the relevant assets are located.

Who pays withholding tax under a cross-border contract?

This depends on the applicable laws and contractual arrangement. The contract should clearly address tax responsibilities, withholding and any gross-up obligations where relevant.

Can a domestic contract be used for an international transaction?

It can sometimes serve as a starting point, but it should not be assumed to be suitable without reviewing the jurisdictions, governing law, regulatory requirements and cross-border risks.
