International expansion can create significant opportunities for a growing business—but entering a new country also introduces new legal, regulatory, contractual and operational considerations.

A business that is successful in its home market cannot simply assume that the same contracts, structure, policies and practices will work in another jurisdiction.

Before entering a new market, business owners should understand how the target country's legal framework may affect the way the business is established, operated, financed and contracted.

What Should Businesses Consider Before Expanding Internationally?

International expansion should be assessed from both a commercial and legal perspective.

Key areas include:

  • Market-entry structure
  • Licensing and regulatory requirements
  • Corporate structure
  • Contracts
  • Employment
  • Tax
  • Intellectual property
  • Data protection
  • Dispute resolution
  • Local partners and suppliers
  • Cross-border payments

The exact requirements will depend on the countries involved, the business activity and the proposed structure.

1. Choose the Right Market-Entry Structure

One of the first decisions is determining how the business will enter the new market.

Options may include:

  • Establishing a local company
  • Opening a branch
  • Working through a distributor
  • Appointing an agent
  • Entering into a joint venture
  • Partnering with an existing business
  • Providing services cross-border without establishing locally, where permitted

Each structure can have different implications for control, liability, licensing, taxation and compliance.

The cheapest structure is not necessarily the most appropriate one.

2. Understand Local Licensing and Regulatory Requirements

Before commencing operations, determine whether the proposed activity requires:

  • A specific business licence
  • Regulatory approval
  • Professional registration
  • Sector-specific authorisation
  • Local representation
  • Additional permits

Certain industries—including financial services, healthcare, education and other regulated sectors—may have substantially more complex requirements.

Regulatory requirements should be assessed before committing significant capital to a new market.

3. Review Local Corporate Requirements

If establishing a local entity, understand the requirements relating to:

  • Ownership
  • Directors and management
  • Corporate governance
  • Registered office
  • Reporting
  • Record keeping
  • Beneficial ownership
  • Local filings

These requirements can differ significantly between jurisdictions.

4. Review Your International Contracts

Existing contracts may not automatically be suitable for international operations.

Review:

  • Governing law
  • Jurisdiction
  • Payment terms
  • Currency
  • Liability
  • Indemnities
  • Confidentiality
  • Intellectual property
  • Termination
  • Force majeure
  • Dispute resolution

Contracts should clearly establish which law applies and how disputes will be handled.

5. Protect Your Intellectual Property

International expansion can expose intellectual property to additional risks.

Consider protecting:

  • Trademarks
  • Copyright
  • Patents
  • Trade secrets
  • Software
  • Brand assets
  • Proprietary information

Intellectual property protection is generally territorial, meaning protection in one country may not automatically provide equivalent protection elsewhere.

6. Understand Employment Laws

Hiring employees in another country introduces local legal obligations.

These may relate to:

  • Employment contracts
  • Working hours
  • Compensation
  • Leave
  • Benefits
  • Termination
  • Immigration
  • Employee rights

Businesses should avoid assuming that employment practices from their home jurisdiction can simply be replicated internationally.

7. Consider Tax and Cross-Border Financial Issues

International operations can create additional tax and financial considerations.

Depending on the structure, businesses may need to consider:

  • Corporate taxation
  • Withholding taxes
  • VAT or indirect taxes
  • Transfer pricing
  • Permanent establishment
  • Cross-border payments
  • Double taxation
  • Currency considerations

Tax structuring should be considered alongside the legal structure rather than after the expansion has already begun.

8. Address Data Protection and Privacy

Businesses transferring customer, employee or commercial information across borders should understand applicable data protection requirements.

Consider:

  • What data is collected?
  • Where is it stored?
  • Who can access it?
  • Where is it transferred?
  • Are third-party processors involved?
  • What privacy obligations apply?

Data protection requirements can differ substantially between jurisdictions.

9. Assess Local Partners and Joint Ventures

International expansion often involves local partners, distributors, agents or joint-venture partners.

Before entering into such relationships, conduct appropriate legal and commercial due diligence.

Review:

  • Ownership
  • Reputation
  • Financial position
  • Regulatory standing
  • Existing commitments
  • Conflicts of interest
  • Decision-making rights

A strong partner can accelerate expansion.

The wrong partner can create significant legal and commercial exposure.

10. Decide How Disputes Will Be Resolved

Cross-border disputes can become complicated when parties operate in different jurisdictions.

Contracts should consider:

  • Governing law
  • Court jurisdiction
  • Arbitration
  • Arbitration seat
  • Enforcement
  • Mediation
  • Dispute escalation procedures

For international transactions, a well-structured dispute resolution clause can be particularly important.

11. Review Your Existing Corporate and Legal Structure

Before expanding, consider whether the existing group structure is suitable for international growth.

Questions may include:

  • Should a new subsidiary be established?
  • Should intellectual property be held separately?
  • How will contracts be entered into?
  • How will funds move between entities?
  • Who will bear operational risk?
  • How will management control be maintained?

The right structure can help manage risk, governance and future expansion.

12. Do Not Assume One Legal Framework Fits Every Market

One of the most common mistakes in international expansion is treating different jurisdictions as if they operate under the same rules.

A contract, employment structure, licence or corporate arrangement that works in one country may require significant modification elsewhere.

International expansion should therefore involve local legal review in the relevant jurisdictions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What legal issues should a business consider before expanding internationally?

Key considerations include market-entry structure, licensing, corporate requirements, contracts, tax, employment, intellectual property, data protection, local partners and dispute resolution.

Does a company need to establish a local entity when expanding internationally?

Not always. Depending on the jurisdiction and business activity, a company may be able to operate through cross-border services, a distributor, agent, branch, subsidiary or joint venture. Local legal and regulatory requirements should be assessed first.

Why are international contracts different from domestic contracts?

International contracts involve additional considerations such as governing law, jurisdiction, currency, cross-border payments, enforcement, tax, liability and international dispute resolution.

How can a business protect its intellectual property internationally?

Businesses should assess protection requirements in each relevant jurisdiction for trademarks, patents, copyright, trade secrets and other intellectual property.

What should businesses consider when selecting an international partner?

Businesses should conduct appropriate due diligence covering the partner's ownership, reputation, financial position, regulatory standing, experience and potential conflicts of interest.

Why is dispute resolution important in international contracts?

If parties are located in different jurisdictions, determining which law applies, where disputes will be resolved and how decisions will be enforced can become critical.
