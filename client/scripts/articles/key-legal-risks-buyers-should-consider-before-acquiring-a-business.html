Acquiring a business is often presented as a financial opportunity: strong revenue, established customers, valuable assets, or potential for growth.

But what you are buying legally can be just as important as what you are buying financially.

A business may look attractive on paper while carrying contractual obligations, disputes, regulatory issues, ownership problems, or other liabilities that could affect its value after completion.

For buyers, the objective of legal due diligence is therefore not simply to identify problems. It is to understand which legal risks could affect the transaction and how those risks should be addressed before the deal is completed.

What Legal Risks Should Buyers Look For?

The risks will vary depending on the business and transaction structure, but the following areas deserve particular attention.

1. Unclear Ownership or Corporate Structure

Before acquiring a company, the buyer should establish who legally owns the business and what exactly is being transferred.

Potential issues may include:

  • Unclear share ownership
  • Existing shareholder arrangements
  • Restrictions on transferring shares
  • Undisclosed ownership interests
  • Corporate approvals required for the transaction

A buyer should be confident that the seller has the legal authority to sell what is being offered.

2. Undisclosed Liabilities

A company can carry obligations that are not immediately obvious from its financial performance.

These may include:

  • Contractual obligations
  • Guarantees
  • Security arrangements
  • Outstanding claims
  • Regulatory liabilities
  • Long-term commitments

This is one reason legal due diligence should be considered alongside financial due diligence.

A profitable business can still carry significant legal exposure.

3. Problematic Commercial Contracts

A business may depend heavily on a small number of customers, suppliers, distributors, landlords, or strategic partners.

Buyers should therefore review material contracts to determine whether they contain:

  • Change-of-control provisions
  • Assignment restrictions
  • Termination rights
  • Automatic renewal
  • Exclusivity obligations
  • Unusual liabilities

A key customer contract that can be terminated following an acquisition could materially affect the value of the business.

4. Existing or Potential Litigation

Current disputes are not the only concern.

Buyers should also consider whether there are circumstances that could lead to future claims.

This may include:

  • Court proceedings
  • Arbitration
  • Regulatory investigations
  • Customer disputes
  • Employment claims
  • Contractual disagreements
  • Threatened claims

The important question is not simply "Is the company being sued?"

It is:

"What legal disputes or circumstances could become the buyer's problem after the acquisition?"

5. Regulatory and Licensing Issues

The business must have the licences, approvals and registrations necessary to operate its activities.

Potential concerns may include:

  • Expired licences
  • Missing approvals
  • Regulatory non-compliance
  • Restrictions on activities
  • Sector-specific requirements
  • Conditions attached to licences

This is particularly important when acquiring a business operating in a regulated industry.

6. Intellectual Property Ownership

Intellectual property can be a significant part of a company's value.

Buyers should establish whether the business actually owns or has appropriate rights to use:

  • Trademarks
  • Software
  • Copyright
  • Patents
  • Domain names
  • Proprietary content
  • Technology

A business valuation may assume ownership of valuable IP that, legally, the company does not fully own.

The value of an asset means little if the legal right to use it is uncertain.

7. Employment and Management Risks

Employees and key management can be critical to the continuity of an acquired business.

Legal review may identify:

  • Employment claims
  • Outstanding employee entitlements
  • Senior management agreements
  • Restrictive covenants
  • Termination obligations
  • Employee-related disputes

Buyers should also understand what happens to key personnel following the transaction.

8. Property, Lease and Asset Risks

Where the business depends on physical premises or significant assets, their legal status should be examined.

This can include:

  • Ownership of key assets
  • Commercial leases
  • Restrictions on transfer
  • Security interests
  • Equipment agreements
  • Landlord consent requirements

A business operating successfully from a particular location may face disruption if its lease cannot continue after the transaction.

9. Tax and Regulatory Exposure

Legal and tax issues can overlap during an acquisition.

Buyers should understand whether there are:

  • Outstanding tax obligations
  • Regulatory penalties
  • Unresolved compliance matters
  • Government claims
  • Historical obligations

These matters should be coordinated with the appropriate financial and tax due diligence.

10. Related-Party Transactions

Transactions between the target company and its owners, directors, shareholders or related entities deserve careful attention.

Examples include:

  • Loans from shareholders
  • Related-party service agreements
  • Shared employees or resources
  • Related-party leases
  • Intercompany arrangements

These relationships may need to be terminated, transferred or restructured following the acquisition.

Not Every Legal Risk Is a Deal Breaker

Finding a legal issue during due diligence does not automatically mean the buyer should walk away.

The more important question is:

How significant is the risk, and can it be managed?

Depending on the circumstances, the buyer may seek:

  • Remediation before completion
  • A purchase price adjustment
  • Specific warranties
  • Indemnities
  • Escrow or retention arrangements
  • Contractual protections
  • Transaction restructuring

Some risks may be acceptable if they are properly understood and commercially justified.

Legal Risk Can Affect Business Value

One of the most important aspects of acquisition due diligence is understanding the connection between legal risk and valuation.

For example:

A business may have strong recurring revenue, but if its key customer contracts can be terminated after a change of control, the sustainability of that revenue may need to be reconsidered.

Similarly, a company may appear to own valuable intellectual property, but uncertainty around ownership could affect the buyer's assessment of the business.

Legal risk is therefore not separate from commercial value. It can directly influence it.

What Should Buyers Do Before Signing?

Before committing to an acquisition, buyers should have a clear understanding of:

  • What they are acquiring
  • Who legally owns it
  • What contracts support the business
  • What liabilities may transfer
  • Whether the business is properly licensed
  • Whether there are existing or potential disputes
  • What intellectual property is actually owned
  • What obligations will continue after completion

The purpose is not to create unnecessary obstacles.

It is to make sure the buyer is making the investment decision with a clear understanding of the legal position.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main legal risks when buying a business?

Key risks include ownership issues, undisclosed liabilities, problematic contracts, litigation, regulatory non-compliance, intellectual property issues, employment liabilities, property and lease issues, tax exposure and related-party arrangements.

Can legal risks reduce the value of a business?

Yes. Significant legal risks can affect the sustainability of revenue, future costs, transaction terms and ultimately the buyer's assessment of the company's value.

Should buyers review contracts before acquiring a business?

Yes. Material customer, supplier, lease, financing and strategic contracts should be reviewed to identify termination, assignment, change-of-control and other provisions that could affect the business after acquisition.

Does every legal problem mean the buyer should walk away?

No. Many risks can potentially be managed through remediation, warranties, indemnities, price adjustments or other transaction protections.

When should legal due diligence take place?

Ideally, legal due diligence should begin early enough to allow the buyer to investigate issues and negotiate appropriate protections before the transaction becomes irreversible.
