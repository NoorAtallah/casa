Buying a business is not simply a financial decision. A company may appear profitable, have strong customers and operate successfully, yet still carry legal risks that could affect its value, operations or future.

This is why legal due diligence is an important part of the acquisition process.

For a buyer, the objective is not to find reasons not to proceed. It is to understand what exactly is being acquired, what obligations come with it, and what risks should be addressed before the transaction is completed.

What Is Legal Due Diligence?

Legal due diligence is the process of reviewing a company's legal affairs before an investment, acquisition or other significant transaction.

The review typically examines areas such as:

  • Corporate structure and ownership
  • Commercial contracts
  • Licences and regulatory compliance
  • Employment matters
  • Litigation and disputes
  • Intellectual property
  • Real estate and leases
  • Loans and financial obligations
  • Material liabilities
  • Related-party arrangements

The scope depends on the nature, size and complexity of the business and the proposed transaction.

Why Does Legal Due Diligence Matter?

A buyer is not only purchasing a company's assets and revenue.

Depending on the transaction structure, the buyer may also inherit contracts, obligations, disputes, liabilities, regulatory issues and other legal exposures.

A legal due diligence process can help answer important questions:

Does the seller actually own what is being sold?

Are the company's key contracts secure and transferable?

Are there undisclosed legal liabilities?

Is the business properly licensed and compliant?

Could existing disputes affect the company's future?

These answers can influence the purchase price, transaction structure, warranties, indemnities and whether the buyer proceeds with the transaction at all.

What Should Buyers Review During Legal Due Diligence?

1. Corporate Structure and Ownership

The first step is understanding who owns the business and what exactly is being acquired.

This may include reviewing:

  • Constitutional documents
  • Shareholding structure
  • Shareholder agreements
  • Corporate registrations
  • Board and shareholder resolutions
  • Existing ownership rights or restrictions

A buyer should be confident that the seller has the legal authority to transfer the relevant shares or assets.

2. Material Commercial Contracts

Key contracts can represent a significant part of a company's value.

Review may include:

  • Major customer agreements
  • Supplier contracts
  • Distribution agreements
  • Partnership arrangements
  • Service agreements
  • Financing arrangements
  • Technology contracts

The buyer should consider whether important contracts contain termination, assignment, change-of-control or renewal provisions that could be triggered by the transaction.

A business may look attractive until the buyer discovers that its most important customer contract can be terminated following a change in ownership.

3. Licences and Regulatory Compliance

The buyer should understand whether the business has the licences, approvals and registrations required to operate.

Depending on the business, this may include:

  • Trade and commercial licences
  • Sector-specific approvals
  • Regulatory registrations
  • Permits
  • Compliance obligations

For regulated businesses, this area can be particularly important.

4. Litigation and Disputes

Existing or threatened disputes can create significant exposure for a buyer.

Due diligence should consider:

  • Current litigation
  • Arbitration
  • Regulatory investigations
  • Claims and complaints
  • Settlement agreements
  • Potential or threatened disputes

The key question is not only "Is the company currently being sued?"

It is also:

"Are there circumstances that could reasonably develop into a legal claim?"

5. Employment and Workforce Matters

Employees can represent both an important asset and a potential legal liability.

Depending on the transaction, buyers may need to review:

  • Employment contracts
  • Senior management arrangements
  • Outstanding employee claims
  • Benefits and entitlements
  • Restrictive covenants
  • Termination obligations
  • Employment-related disputes

The treatment of employees can also be affected by the structure of the transaction.

6. Intellectual Property

For many businesses, intellectual property is a significant component of value.

Review may include:

  • Trademarks
  • Copyright
  • Patents
  • Software
  • Domain names
  • Proprietary content
  • Licences
  • Third-party IP arrangements

The key question is:

Does the company actually own or have the right to use the intellectual property that is critical to its business?

7. Assets, Property and Leases

Where physical assets or premises are important to the business, buyers should understand the legal basis on which they are held or used.

This may include:

  • Ownership of key assets
  • Commercial leases
  • Property rights
  • Equipment arrangements
  • Security interests
  • Restrictions on transfer

A lease or key asset agreement may contain provisions that become relevant following a change in ownership.

8. Existing Liabilities and Obligations

A company may have obligations that are not immediately obvious from its financial statements.

Legal due diligence can help identify:

  • Guarantees
  • Security arrangements
  • Long-term contractual commitments
  • Unusual obligations
  • Related-party agreements
  • Potential claims
  • Regulatory exposure

This is where legal due diligence and financial due diligence complement each other.

Financial statements tell you what has been recorded. Legal due diligence can help identify obligations and risks that may require further investigation.

What Happens If Due Diligence Finds a Problem?

Finding a legal issue does not automatically mean the transaction should be abandoned.

Depending on the nature of the issue, the buyer may:

  • Request additional information
  • Ask the seller to resolve the issue before completion
  • Negotiate a reduction in the purchase price
  • Request specific warranties
  • Seek an indemnity
  • Restructure the transaction
  • Retain part of the purchase price
  • Accept the risk where commercially justified
  • Decide not to proceed

This is why due diligence should not be viewed simply as a checklist exercise.

Its real value comes from using the findings to make a better transaction decision.

Legal Due Diligence vs. Financial Due Diligence

The two processes serve different purposes but should work together.

Financial due diligence focuses primarily on the financial performance, position and sustainability of the business.

Legal due diligence examines the company's legal structure, contracts, obligations, compliance, disputes, ownership and potential legal exposure.

Neither necessarily replaces the other.

A business can have strong financial performance while carrying significant legal risks—and those risks can affect its value.

When Should Legal Due Diligence Begin?

Ideally, legal due diligence should begin before the transaction becomes irreversible.

Starting early gives the buyer time to:

  • Identify potential issues
  • Request additional documentation
  • Investigate concerns
  • Negotiate transaction terms
  • Address issues before completion

Waiting until the final stages of a transaction can leave little room to negotiate when significant problems are discovered.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is legal due diligence when buying a business?

Legal due diligence is the process of reviewing a target company's legal structure, contracts, ownership, compliance, disputes, intellectual property, employment matters and other legal obligations before an acquisition.

Why is legal due diligence important for buyers?

It helps buyers identify legal risks and obligations that could affect the value, operation, ownership or future of the acquired business.

What documents are reviewed during legal due diligence?

The documents depend on the transaction but may include corporate records, licences, material contracts, employment documents, litigation records, intellectual property documents, leases and financing or security arrangements.

Can legal due diligence affect the purchase price?

Yes. Material legal risks may influence the purchase price, transaction structure, warranties, indemnities or other protections negotiated between the buyer and seller.

Does legal due diligence replace financial due diligence?

No. They address different areas of risk. Financial due diligence assesses the financial aspects of the business, while legal due diligence assesses its legal position and exposure. They are most effective when conducted together.

What happens if legal due diligence identifies a problem?

The buyer can investigate the issue and, depending on its significance, negotiate remediation, a price adjustment, warranties, indemnities, transaction restructuring or other protections.
