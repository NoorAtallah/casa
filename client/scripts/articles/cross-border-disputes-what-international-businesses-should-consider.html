International business relationships can create significant commercial opportunities—but they can also create more complex disputes.

When parties operate in different countries, a disagreement may involve different legal systems, jurisdictions, governing laws, languages, currencies and enforcement mechanisms.

A dispute that might be relatively straightforward domestically can become considerably more complicated when the parties, assets or evidence are located in different jurisdictions.

For this reason, businesses should consider how a potential dispute will be resolved before entering into the relationship, rather than waiting until a dispute occurs.

What Is a Cross-Border Dispute?

A cross-border dispute generally arises when a commercial disagreement involves parties, transactions, assets or obligations connected to more than one jurisdiction.

Examples include:

  • International commercial contracts
  • Cross-border investments
  • Joint ventures
  • Distribution agreements
  • International supply arrangements
  • Shareholder disputes
  • M&A transactions
  • Intellectual property disputes
  • Payment disputes
  • Cross-border employment matters

The complexity often comes not only from the underlying disagreement, but from determining which country's laws and courts or dispute-resolution mechanisms should apply.

1. Start With the Contract

The best time to think about a dispute is before signing the contract.

A well-drafted international agreement should clearly address:

  • Governing law
  • Jurisdiction
  • Arbitration or litigation
  • Arbitration seat, where applicable
  • Notice requirements
  • Liability
  • Indemnities
  • Termination
  • Confidentiality
  • Evidence and documentation

A contract that does not clearly address these matters can create additional uncertainty when a dispute arises.

2. Determine Which Law Applies

One of the first questions in a cross-border dispute is:

Which country's law governs the relationship?

The answer may affect how the contract is interpreted, the parties' rights and obligations, available remedies and the enforceability of certain provisions.

Businesses should therefore consider the governing law clause carefully when negotiating international contracts.

Importantly, choosing a governing law does not necessarily eliminate the effect of mandatory laws or public-policy rules that may apply in another jurisdiction.

3. Decide Where the Dispute Will Be Resolved

Governing law and dispute resolution are related, but they are not the same thing.

A contract may, for example, choose the law of one jurisdiction while providing for arbitration in another.

Businesses should therefore determine:

  • Which courts have jurisdiction?
  • Should disputes go to arbitration?
  • Where should arbitration take place?
  • Which procedural rules will apply?
  • How practical will enforcement be?

These decisions can materially affect the cost and complexity of a dispute.

4. Consider Arbitration

International commercial arbitration is frequently used for cross-border disputes because it can provide a structured mechanism for resolving disputes outside national courts.

The UAE has a dedicated federal arbitration law, Federal Law No. 6 of 2018, which regulates arbitration in the UAE.

Internationally, the New York Convention provides a framework for recognition and enforcement of foreign arbitral awards. The UAE has been a party to the Convention since 2006, and the Convention currently has 172 parties.

However, arbitration is not automatically the best solution for every dispute.

The appropriate mechanism depends on factors such as:

  • Nature of the transaction
  • Jurisdictions involved
  • Value of the dispute
  • Confidentiality requirements
  • Enforcement considerations
  • Urgency
  • Costs

5. Consider the Enforceability of the Outcome

Winning a dispute is only part of the process.

The more important question may be:

Can the judgment or award actually be enforced where the counterparty or its assets are located?

Before selecting a dispute-resolution mechanism, businesses should consider where the counterparty's:

  • Bank accounts
  • Property
  • Shares
  • Equipment
  • Receivables
  • Other assets

are located.

An enforceability assessment can be particularly important in significant international transactions.

6. Consider Mediation Before Litigation or Arbitration

Not every commercial disagreement needs to become a full legal battle.

Mediation can provide an opportunity for the parties to reach a negotiated settlement while preserving the commercial relationship.

International mediation can also provide a structured alternative to formal proceedings. The Singapore Convention on Mediation establishes a framework for recognition and enforcement of certain international commercial settlement agreements resulting from mediation.

For some businesses, resolving the dispute commercially may be more valuable than pursuing the maximum legal remedy.

7. Preserve Evidence Early

Cross-border disputes can involve evidence spread across multiple countries.

Businesses should preserve:

  • Contracts
  • Emails
  • Invoices
  • Payment records
  • Correspondence
  • Meeting records
  • Corporate documents
  • Technical documents
  • Digital communications

Evidence can become difficult to obtain or preserve if action is delayed.

Where litigation or arbitration is reasonably foreseeable, businesses should consider obtaining appropriate legal advice on document preservation and evidence management.

8. Consider Language and Translation

International disputes may involve documents in multiple languages.

Businesses should establish:

  • The contractual language
  • The language of proceedings
  • Translation requirements
  • The authenticity of translated documents
  • Whether bilingual contracts are required

Translation issues can create unnecessary complications if they are not addressed early.

9. Assess the Commercial Relationship

Legal strategy should not be separated from commercial reality.

Before escalating a dispute, businesses should consider:

  • Is the counterparty strategically important?
  • Is the relationship worth preserving?
  • Can the dispute be resolved commercially?
  • What will litigation or arbitration cost?
  • How long could the process take?
  • Could the dispute damage the business's reputation?
  • Is there a realistic recovery?

Sometimes the strongest legal strategy is a negotiated commercial solution.

10. Consider Interim Protection

In certain situations, a business may need urgent protection before the final dispute is resolved.

Depending on the applicable legal framework, this could involve measures relating to:

  • Assets
  • Evidence
  • Confidential information
  • Contractual rights
  • Business operations

International arbitration frameworks can also provide mechanisms for interim measures. UNCITRAL's Model Law, for example, addresses interim measures in support of arbitration.

The availability and procedure for such measures depend on the relevant jurisdiction and circumstances.

11. Review the Contract Before Escalating the Dispute

Before commencing proceedings, businesses should carefully review:

  • Governing law
  • Dispute-resolution clause
  • Notice requirements
  • Limitation periods
  • Termination provisions
  • Liability caps
  • Indemnities
  • Payment provisions
  • Evidence requirements

A party may have a strong commercial complaint but still need to follow specific contractual procedures before bringing a claim.

12. Assess the Cost Versus the Potential Recovery

Cross-border disputes can involve significant costs.

Before proceeding, businesses should assess:

Potential recovery vs.

  • Legal fees
  • Arbitration or court fees
  • Expert costs
  • Translation costs
  • Travel
  • Enforcement costs
  • Management time
  • Opportunity cost

The objective is not simply to determine whether the business can bring a claim.

It is to determine whether pursuing the claim is commercially sensible.

The Biggest Mistake Businesses Make

One of the biggest mistakes in international contracting is treating the dispute-resolution clause as standard boilerplate.

It is not.

The choice of:

governing law + jurisdiction/arbitration + enforcement strategy

can significantly influence what happens if the relationship breaks down.

These provisions should therefore be negotiated with the commercial and legal consequences in mind.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a cross-border commercial dispute?

A cross-border commercial dispute is a business disagreement involving parties, transactions, assets or obligations connected to more than one jurisdiction.

What are the main issues in a cross-border dispute?

Key issues include governing law, jurisdiction, arbitration or litigation, enforcement, evidence, language, costs and the location of the counterparty's assets.

Is arbitration better than litigation for international disputes?

Not necessarily. Arbitration can be useful for certain international disputes, particularly where enforcement and neutrality are important, but the appropriate mechanism depends on the transaction and jurisdictions involved.

What is the difference between governing law and jurisdiction?

Governing law determines which legal rules generally apply to the dispute. Jurisdiction determines which court or tribunal has authority to hear it.

Why is enforcement important in an international dispute?

A successful judgment or arbitral award may have limited practical value if it cannot be effectively enforced where the counterparty or its assets are located.

Can international business disputes be resolved through mediation?

Yes. Mediation can provide a negotiated alternative to litigation or arbitration and may help preserve the underlying commercial relationship.

Should a business review its dispute-resolution clause before signing an international contract?

Yes. The dispute-resolution clause can have significant consequences for cost, jurisdiction, procedure, enforcement and the overall strategy if a dispute occurs.

Does the UAE recognise foreign arbitral awards?

The UAE is a party to the New York Convention, which provides an international framework for recognition and enforcement of foreign arbitral awards.
