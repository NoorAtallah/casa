Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) can create significant opportunities for business owners—from entering new markets and expanding capabilities to unlocking the value they have built over time.

But an acquisition is not simply a transaction of buying or selling shares.

It involves strategy, valuation, due diligence, negotiation, legal documentation and careful execution.

For business owners considering an acquisition, merger or sale, understanding the process early can help avoid costly surprises and protect the value of the transaction.

What Are Mergers and Acquisitions?

Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) refer broadly to transactions where businesses combine, one business acquires another, or ownership of a business changes.

Common structures include:

  • Share acquisitions
  • Asset acquisitions
  • Business transfers
  • Mergers
  • Joint ventures and strategic combinations
  • Management or investor-led acquisitions

The appropriate structure depends on the objectives of the parties, the business, its assets and liabilities, and the applicable legal and regulatory framework.

Why Do Businesses Pursue M&A?

An acquisition may allow a business to:

  • Enter a new market
  • Expand its customer base
  • Acquire technology or intellectual property
  • Access new capabilities
  • Increase market share
  • Achieve operational efficiencies
  • Diversify revenue
  • Accelerate growth

For sellers, an M&A transaction may provide an opportunity to realise the value created in the business or bring in a strategic partner for the next stage of growth.

The strategic rationale should be clear before the transaction begins.

What Does the M&A Process Involve?

Although every transaction is different, a typical M&A process includes several key stages.

1. Define the Transaction Strategy

Before approaching a buyer or target, business owners should establish:

  • Why the transaction is being considered
  • What the desired outcome is
  • Which businesses or investors are appropriate
  • What value the transaction should create
  • What risks the business is prepared to accept

A clear strategy helps prevent the transaction from becoming driven purely by price.

2. Initial Assessment and Valuation

Before entering detailed negotiations, the parties need an understanding of the business's potential value.

Factors can include:

  • Financial performance
  • Revenue quality
  • Profitability
  • Assets
  • Intellectual property
  • Customer concentration
  • Market position
  • Growth potential
  • Existing liabilities

A valuation is not necessarily a single fixed number. The structure and terms of the transaction can also influence the overall value received.

3. Confidentiality and Initial Negotiations

Once a potential buyer or target has been identified, discussions normally begin under appropriate confidentiality arrangements.

Depending on the transaction, the parties may enter into:

  • Non-disclosure agreements
  • Heads of terms
  • Letters of intent
  • Exclusivity arrangements

These documents help establish the framework for negotiations before the parties commit to definitive agreements.

4. Due Diligence

Due diligence allows the buyer to investigate the business before completing the transaction.

It can cover:

Legal → Financial → Tax → Commercial → Operational → Regulatory

Legal due diligence may examine:

  • Corporate structure
  • Ownership
  • Material contracts
  • Litigation
  • Employment
  • Intellectual property
  • Licences
  • Liabilities

The purpose is not simply to identify problems.

It is to understand what the buyer is actually acquiring and how identified risks should be addressed.

5. Negotiating the Transaction

The findings from due diligence can influence the commercial terms.

Negotiations may cover:

  • Purchase price
  • Payment structure
  • Warranties
  • Indemnities
  • Conditions precedent
  • Completion requirements
  • Management arrangements
  • Non-compete provisions
  • Post-completion obligations

A strong negotiation focuses on both price and risk allocation.

6. Transaction Documentation

Once the key commercial terms are agreed, the parties move towards definitive legal documentation.

Depending on the structure, this may include:

  • Share Purchase Agreement
  • Asset Purchase Agreement
  • Shareholders' Agreement
  • Disclosure Letter
  • Employment or consultancy agreements
  • Transitional services arrangements
  • Other ancillary documents

The documents should reflect the commercial agreement and appropriately allocate identified risks.

7. Completion and Post-Completion

The transaction is completed once the agreed conditions have been satisfied and the required documents and consideration have been exchanged.

But completion is not necessarily the end of the process.

Post-completion matters may include:

  • Transfer of ownership
  • Regulatory notifications
  • Integration
  • Employee transition
  • Customer and supplier communication
  • Operational restructuring
  • Post-completion obligations

For acquisitions, integration planning should begin well before completion.

What Are the Main Legal Risks in M&A?

Business owners should pay particular attention to:

Ownership

Is the seller legally entitled to transfer the shares or assets?

Contracts

Could important agreements terminate or change following the transaction?

Liabilities

What existing or potential obligations could affect the buyer?

Intellectual Property

Does the company actually own its key IP?

Litigation

Are there existing or potential disputes?

Regulatory Compliance

Does the business have the required licences and approvals?

Employees

Are there employment liabilities or key personnel considerations?

Transaction Structure

Does the chosen structure appropriately allocate assets, liabilities and risk?

These issues can directly affect value, negotiation and transaction certainty.

Should You Buy Shares or Assets?

One of the important decisions in an acquisition is whether to acquire the shares of the company or selected assets/business operations.

A share acquisition generally involves acquiring the company together with its existing legal and commercial relationships.

An asset acquisition can allow the buyer to select specific assets or business components, depending on the structure.

There is no universally better option.

The appropriate structure depends on the commercial objectives, liabilities, regulatory considerations, tax implications and legal position of the target.

Professional advice should be obtained before selecting the structure.

What Can Make an M&A Transaction Go Wrong?

Common problems include:

  • Inadequate due diligence
  • Unrealistic valuation expectations
  • Poorly defined transaction objectives
  • Undisclosed liabilities
  • Weak contractual protections
  • Failure to identify change-of-control provisions
  • Poor negotiation of warranties and indemnities
  • Lack of integration planning
  • Misalignment between buyer and seller

Many of these issues can be identified and managed before signing.

M&A Is About More Than the Purchase Price

A headline purchase price does not tell the whole story.

Business owners should also consider:

  • Payment timing
  • Deferred consideration
  • Earn-outs
  • Retention arrangements
  • Warranties
  • Indemnities
  • Continuing obligations
  • Management involvement
  • Post-completion restrictions

The right transaction is one that creates appropriate value while allocating risk fairly and protecting the parties' interests.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are mergers and acquisitions?

Mergers and acquisitions are transactions involving the combination, acquisition or transfer of businesses, shares or business assets.

What are the main stages of an M&A transaction?

A typical M&A process involves strategy, valuation, initial negotiations, confidentiality, due diligence, negotiation, transaction documentation, completion and post-completion activities.

What is legal due diligence in an acquisition?

Legal due diligence involves reviewing the target's ownership, contracts, liabilities, litigation, intellectual property, employment matters, licences and other legal risks before completion.

Should I buy the shares or assets of a business?

It depends on the transaction. Share and asset acquisitions have different legal, commercial, regulatory and financial implications. The appropriate structure should be assessed based on the specific circumstances.

How long does an M&A transaction take?

There is no standard timeframe. The duration depends on the size and complexity of the transaction, due diligence requirements, negotiations, regulatory approvals and transaction structure.

What should business owners do before selling their company?

Owners should consider preparing the company for sale by reviewing its legal structure, contracts, financial position, intellectual property, liabilities and regulatory compliance before approaching potential buyers.
