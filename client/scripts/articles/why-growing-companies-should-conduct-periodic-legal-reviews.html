Growth is one of the most important milestones for any business—but it also changes the company's legal and commercial exposure.

As a company grows, it typically takes on more employees, customers, suppliers, contracts, shareholders, assets and regulatory obligations. Documents and arrangements that worked when the business was smaller may no longer provide adequate protection.

A periodic legal review helps business owners identify these changes, address emerging risks and ensure the company's legal framework continues to support its growth.

The objective is not to create unnecessary legal work. It is to identify issues early, before they become expensive or disruptive.

What Is a Periodic Legal Review?

A periodic legal review is a structured assessment of a company's key legal documents, obligations, relationships and potential areas of exposure.

It may cover:

  • Corporate structure and governance
  • Commercial contracts
  • Employment arrangements
  • Regulatory compliance
  • Intellectual property
  • Data protection
  • Disputes and claims
  • Shareholder arrangements
  • Key customers and suppliers
  • Business expansion and future transactions

The scope should be proportionate to the size, industry, structure and stage of the business.

Why Does Legal Review Become More Important as a Company Grows?

A growing company is not simply a larger version of the same business.

Growth often introduces new risks, new relationships and new obligations.

For example, a company may move from a founder-led operation to one with multiple shareholders, senior management, significant contracts and operations across different markets.

The legal framework needs to evolve with it.

1. Your Contracts May No Longer Reflect Your Business

As businesses grow, they often continue using contracts that were prepared when the company was much smaller.

These agreements may not adequately address:

  • Larger transaction values
  • New products or services
  • Increased liability
  • Intellectual property
  • Data protection
  • Service levels
  • Termination
  • Dispute resolution

A periodic review can identify contracts that need to be updated or replaced.

2. Your Corporate Structure May Need Strengthening

Growth can introduce new shareholders, investors, directors and management structures.

This makes it important to review:

  • Shareholder agreements
  • Voting rights
  • Decision-making authority
  • Board responsibilities
  • Delegation of authority
  • Corporate records
  • Ownership arrangements

Clear governance can become increasingly important as more people become involved in the business.

3. New Employees Create New Legal Obligations

Growth usually means hiring.

As the workforce expands, businesses should review whether their employment documentation and internal policies adequately address:

  • Roles and responsibilities
  • Confidentiality
  • Intellectual property
  • Compensation and benefits
  • Working arrangements
  • Termination
  • Employee conduct

Employment arrangements should evolve alongside the workforce.

4. Regulatory Exposure Can Increase

A growing company may enter new sectors, locations or markets.

This can introduce additional:

  • Licensing requirements
  • Regulatory obligations
  • Reporting requirements
  • Data protection responsibilities
  • Industry-specific compliance requirements

What was appropriate for the company at an earlier stage may no longer be sufficient.

5. Intellectual Property Becomes More Valuable

As a company grows, its brand, technology, content, processes and know-how can become significant assets.

A legal review can help determine whether:

  • Intellectual property is properly owned
  • Trademarks are adequately protected
  • Employee and contractor IP is addressed
  • Confidential information is protected
  • Licensing arrangements are appropriate

Protecting intellectual property becomes particularly important when the business begins expanding or attracting investors.

6. Shareholder Relationships May Become More Complex

Growth may involve new shareholders, investors or strategic partners.

This can create questions around:

  • Ownership
  • Voting
  • Dividends
  • Transfers
  • Minority protections
  • Exit rights
  • Deadlock
  • Future investment

These issues are easier to address before a disagreement arises.

7. Commercial Relationships Become More Significant

As revenue increases, businesses often become increasingly dependent on major customers, suppliers and strategic partners.

Review whether key agreements adequately protect the company regarding:

  • Payment
  • Performance
  • Liability
  • Exclusivity
  • Termination
  • Confidentiality
  • Intellectual property
  • Dispute resolution

The larger the commercial relationship, the greater the potential impact if something goes wrong.

8. Growth Can Create Cross-Border Risks

When companies expand internationally, they may encounter different:

  • Laws
  • Licensing requirements
  • Tax considerations
  • Employment rules
  • Contractual standards
  • Data protection requirements
  • Dispute resolution mechanisms

International growth should therefore trigger a review of the company's legal and contractual structure.

9. Legal Readiness Matters Before Investment or Sale

A company does not need to be actively seeking investment or preparing for a sale to benefit from being legally organised.

Potential investors and buyers may review:

  • Corporate records
  • Share ownership
  • Material contracts
  • Intellectual property
  • Employment arrangements
  • Regulatory compliance
  • Litigation
  • Liabilities

Unresolved legal issues can create delays, additional negotiations or even affect the attractiveness and value of a transaction.

10. Early Action Is Usually More Efficient

Legal problems often become more expensive once they escalate.

For example:

Unclear contract → disagreement → dispute → legal proceedings

is usually more costly than:

Contract review → issue identified → clause clarified → risk reduced

A periodic legal review is therefore not simply about compliance.

It is about early risk identification and better business decision-making.

When Should a Growing Company Conduct a Legal Review?

A legal review can be particularly valuable when:

  • Revenue or operations are growing significantly
  • New shareholders or investors are joining
  • The company is entering a new market
  • The workforce is expanding
  • Major contracts are being signed
  • New products or services are launched
  • The company is acquiring another business
  • The business is preparing for investment
  • The owner is considering a future sale
  • The company has experienced a dispute or regulatory issue

Businesses may also benefit from establishing a regular review cycle, rather than waiting for a major event.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a periodic legal review?

A periodic legal review is a structured assessment of a company's contracts, corporate arrangements, compliance obligations, employment documentation, intellectual property and other legal risks.

Why should growing companies conduct legal reviews?

Growth creates new employees, contracts, shareholders, customers, suppliers and regulatory obligations. A legal review helps ensure the company's legal framework keeps pace with its development.

How often should a company conduct a legal review?

There is no universal schedule. The appropriate frequency depends on the company's size, industry, activities, structure and level of risk. Companies experiencing significant growth or change may benefit from more frequent reviews.

What should be reviewed during a business legal review?

Key areas typically include corporate governance, contracts, employment, compliance, intellectual property, data protection, disputes, shareholder arrangements and material commercial relationships.

Can a legal review help before an investment or business sale?

Yes. A legal review can identify issues that may arise during due diligence, including incomplete records, ownership issues, contract weaknesses, regulatory concerns and unresolved liabilities.

Is a legal review only necessary when there is a legal problem?

No. The purpose of a periodic review is to identify potential issues before they become legal or commercial problems.
