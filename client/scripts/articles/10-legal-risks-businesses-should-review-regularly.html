Legal risk is not limited to lawsuits or regulatory penalties.

For business owners, legal risk can arise from contracts, employees, shareholders, customers, suppliers, intellectual property, compliance and even outdated corporate documents.

As a business grows, its legal exposure changes. Agreements that were appropriate when the company was smaller may no longer adequately protect it, while new operations may introduce risks that were not previously relevant.

A regular legal review can help identify these issues before they become expensive problems.

Why Should Businesses Conduct Regular Legal Reviews?

Many business owners only seek legal advice when a problem has already occurred.

A proactive review allows the business to ask:

What could create legal or commercial exposure if we do nothing?

Regular reviews can help businesses:

  • Identify contractual weaknesses
  • Reduce regulatory exposure
  • Protect company assets
  • Clarify ownership and responsibilities
  • Prepare for growth
  • Strengthen negotiation positions
  • Reduce unexpected disputes

The frequency of a legal review should depend on the size, industry, structure and level of activity of the business.

10 Legal Risks Businesses Should Review

1. Contractual Risks

Contracts are among the most significant sources of legal exposure.

Review whether key agreements adequately address:

  • Scope of services
  • Payment terms
  • Liability
  • Indemnities
  • Confidentiality
  • Termination
  • Dispute resolution

An unclear or poorly drafted contract can create significant exposure when a commercial relationship breaks down.

2. Corporate Governance Risks

As businesses grow, informal decision-making can create problems.

Review:

  • Shareholder arrangements
  • Decision-making authority
  • Board and shareholder resolutions
  • Corporate records
  • Delegation of authority
  • Conflicts of interest

Clear governance helps ensure that important decisions are properly authorised and documented.

3. Regulatory and Compliance Risks

Businesses should regularly assess whether they remain compliant with the laws and regulations applicable to their activities.

Depending on the business, this may include:

  • Licensing
  • Corporate filings
  • Industry regulations
  • Data protection
  • Anti-money laundering requirements
  • Employment obligations
  • Tax-related requirements

Compliance should be treated as an ongoing responsibility, not a one-time exercise.

4. Employment and Workforce Risks

Employment-related disputes can arise from unclear contracts, policies or procedures.

Review:

  • Employment agreements
  • Job responsibilities
  • Confidentiality obligations
  • Restrictive covenants
  • Leave and benefits
  • Termination procedures
  • Employee policies

Businesses should ensure their employment documentation remains appropriate as the workforce evolves.

5. Intellectual Property Risks

A company's intellectual property can be one of its most valuable assets.

Review protection and ownership of:

  • Trademarks
  • Copyright
  • Software
  • Designs
  • Trade secrets
  • Proprietary information

Businesses should also confirm that intellectual property created by employees or contractors is appropriately addressed contractually.

6. Data Protection and Confidentiality Risks

Businesses increasingly hold significant amounts of customer, employee and commercial data.

Review:

  • Privacy policies
  • Data collection practices
  • Data storage
  • Third-party access
  • Confidentiality agreements
  • Data-sharing arrangements
  • Data breach procedures

The objective is to ensure that information is being collected, used, stored and shared appropriately.

7. Dispute and Litigation Risks

Existing disputes are not the only concern.

Businesses should identify potential disputes before they escalate.

Review:

  • Customer complaints
  • Supplier disagreements
  • Employee disputes
  • Outstanding claims
  • Contract breaches
  • Threatened litigation
  • Unresolved commercial issues

Early intervention can sometimes prevent a manageable disagreement from becoming a significant legal dispute.

8. Customer and Supplier Risks

A business may be exposed through its relationships with third parties.

Consider whether:

  • Contracts are current
  • Responsibilities are clearly defined
  • Service levels are documented
  • Payment obligations are clear
  • Liability is appropriately allocated
  • Termination rights are adequate

Businesses should pay particular attention to critical customers and suppliers on whom operations heavily depend.

9. Regulatory and Cross-Border Risks

Businesses operating internationally or dealing with foreign counterparties may face additional exposure.

Review:

  • International contracts
  • Governing law
  • Jurisdiction
  • Cross-border payments
  • Local licensing
  • Sanctions and compliance
  • Data transfers
  • International dispute resolution

A structure that works domestically may not automatically be suitable for cross-border operations.

10. Business Sale and Investment Readiness Risks

Even if a sale or investment is not currently planned, businesses should consider whether their legal affairs are transaction-ready.

Potential issues include:

  • Incomplete corporate records
  • Unclear ownership
  • Unresolved disputes
  • Weak contracts
  • Intellectual property gaps
  • Regulatory issues
  • Undocumented liabilities

Addressing these issues early can make future investment, acquisition or sale processes significantly smoother.

A Simple Legal Risk Review Framework

Business owners can periodically review their legal position across five areas:

Contracts → Corporate → Compliance → People → Assets

For each area, ask:

  • What has changed since our last review?
  • Are our documents still appropriate?
  • Are there any unresolved issues?
  • Have new risks emerged?
  • What should be addressed now?

This simple process can help turn legal risk management from a reactive exercise into an ongoing business practice.

When Should a Business Conduct a Legal Review?

A legal review can be particularly valuable when:

  • The business is growing rapidly
  • New shareholders are joining
  • The company is entering a new market
  • Major contracts are being signed
  • The business is hiring significantly
  • The company is acquiring another business
  • New products or services are being launched
  • A dispute has emerged
  • The company is preparing for investment
  • The owner is considering selling the business

However, businesses do not need to wait for a major event.

Periodic legal reviews can identify issues while they are still relatively easy to address.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the most common legal risks for businesses?

Common risks include contractual disputes, corporate governance weaknesses, regulatory non-compliance, employment issues, intellectual property problems, data protection risks and unresolved commercial disputes.

How often should a business conduct a legal review?

There is no single frequency suitable for every business. The appropriate timing depends on the company's size, industry, structure, activities and level of legal exposure. A periodic review is generally preferable to waiting until a problem arises.

Why should growing businesses conduct legal reviews?

Growth often creates new contracts, employees, shareholders, customers, suppliers and regulatory obligations. A legal review helps ensure that the company's legal framework keeps pace with the business.

Can a legal review prevent disputes?

It cannot eliminate all disputes, but identifying unclear contracts, potential conflicts and other weaknesses early can help reduce unnecessary legal exposure and improve the company's position if a dispute arises.

What should a business review before seeking investment?

Businesses should consider reviewing corporate records, ownership, contracts, intellectual property, regulatory compliance, employment arrangements, disputes and material liabilities.

Is a legal review only necessary for large companies?

No. Smaller businesses can also face significant legal exposure. The review should simply be proportionate to the size and complexity of the business.
