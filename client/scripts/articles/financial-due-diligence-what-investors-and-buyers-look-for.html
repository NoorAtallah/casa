When an investor or buyer is considering a business, the question is not simply “Is this business profitable?”

They want to understand whether the financial performance presented by the owner is accurate, sustainable and capable of supporting future growth.

This is the purpose of financial due diligence.

Financial due diligence helps investors and buyers examine the company's financial performance, cash flow, assets, liabilities, risks and underlying assumptions before making a significant investment or acquisition decision.

For business owners, understanding what will be examined can make the transaction more efficient, credible and predictable.

What Is Financial Due Diligence?

Financial due diligence is a structured review of a company's financial information and underlying financial position.

It typically goes beyond reviewing the annual financial statements.

Investors and buyers may examine:

  • Historical financial performance
  • Revenue and profitability
  • Cash flow
  • Working capital
  • Debt and liabilities
  • Assets
  • Customer concentration
  • Financial forecasts
  • Tax and accounting matters
  • One-off or unusual transactions

The objective is to determine whether the financial story presented by the business is supported by the underlying numbers.

What Do Investors and Buyers Look For?

1. Revenue Quality and Sustainability

Buyers want to understand where revenue comes from and whether it is likely to continue.

They may examine:

  • Revenue growth
  • Recurring versus one-off revenue
  • Customer concentration
  • Major customer contracts
  • Revenue by product or service
  • Revenue by market or geography
  • Customer retention

A business with predictable and diversified revenue can generally provide greater confidence than one heavily dependent on a few customers or irregular transactions.

2. Profitability

Investors will look beyond headline net profit.

They may analyse:

  • Gross profit
  • Gross margins
  • EBITDA
  • EBITDA margins
  • Operating expenses
  • Net profit
  • Profit trends

They will also want to understand why profitability has changed.

For example, a sudden increase in profit may require further explanation if it resulted from a one-off transaction rather than sustainable operational improvement.

3. Quality of Earnings

One of the key objectives of financial due diligence is determining whether reported earnings accurately reflect the underlying performance of the business.

The review may identify:

  • One-off income
  • Non-recurring expenses
  • Unusual transactions
  • Owner-related expenses
  • Exceptional costs
  • Accounting adjustments

The objective is to understand the normalised earnings of the business.

4. Cash Flow

Profit does not necessarily equal cash.

Buyers may examine:

  • Operating cash flow
  • Cash conversion
  • Working capital requirements
  • Capital expenditure
  • Free cash flow
  • Cash balances

A business generating accounting profits but consistently consuming cash may require closer examination.

5. Working Capital

Working capital can significantly affect the economics of a transaction.

Review areas may include:

  • Accounts receivable
  • Inventory
  • Accounts payable
  • Payment terms
  • Collection periods
  • Supplier terms

Buyers want to understand how much working capital the business needs to operate normally.

6. Debt and Other Liabilities

A financial due diligence review will generally identify the company's financial obligations.

These may include:

  • Bank loans
  • Shareholder loans
  • Leases
  • Supplier liabilities
  • Accrued expenses
  • Tax liabilities
  • Contingent liabilities

Unidentified or underestimated liabilities can materially affect transaction value.

7. Assets and Balance Sheet Quality

The balance sheet should accurately represent the company's financial position.

Potential areas of review include:

  • Fixed assets
  • Inventory
  • Receivables
  • Cash
  • Intangible assets
  • Provisions
  • Liabilities

Old, unreconciled or unexplained balances can raise concerns during due diligence.

8. Customer Concentration

Investors may examine how dependent the business is on individual customers.

For example:

If one customer represents 40% of revenue, what happens if that customer leaves?

Customer concentration does not automatically make a business unattractive, but it can represent a material risk that needs to be understood and appropriately reflected in the transaction.

9. Financial Forecasts

Historical performance is only part of the picture.

Buyers and investors will often assess whether the company's projections are realistic.

They may challenge:

  • Revenue growth assumptions
  • Pricing assumptions
  • Margin expectations
  • New customer acquisition
  • Operating expenses
  • Capital expenditure
  • Working capital requirements

The stronger the connection between historical performance and future assumptions, the more credible the forecast is likely to be.

10. Accounting and Tax Matters

Financial due diligence may also identify accounting or tax issues that could affect the transaction.

These may include:

  • Unpaid taxes
  • Incorrect classifications
  • Revenue recognition issues
  • Unrecorded liabilities
  • Related-party transactions
  • Incomplete financial records
  • Accounting inconsistencies

These issues do not necessarily prevent a transaction, but they should be identified and understood before completion.

What Financial Due Diligence Can Reveal

A thorough review may uncover:

Positive findings

  • Strong recurring revenue
  • Improving margins
  • Healthy cash generation
  • Efficient working capital
  • Sustainable growth

Potential concerns

  • Overstated profitability
  • Customer concentration
  • Weak cash conversion
  • Unrecorded liabilities
  • One-off income
  • Aggressive financial assumptions

This is why due diligence should not be viewed simply as an exercise to find problems.

It is a process of understanding the true financial position and quality of the business.

What Should Business Owners Prepare?

If you are preparing for an investment or sale, having the following information organised can make the process significantly easier:

  • Historical financial statements
  • Management accounts
  • Bank statements
  • Revenue analysis
  • Customer information
  • Accounts receivable and payable
  • Debt schedules
  • Tax records
  • Fixed asset register
  • Financial forecasts
  • Major contracts
  • Supporting accounting documentation

The exact requirements will depend on the nature and size of the transaction.

How Can Business Owners Prepare for Financial Due Diligence?

The best time to prepare is before the buyer or investor starts asking questions.

Start by:

  • Reviewing your financial statements
  • Reconciling significant balance sheet accounts
  • Identifying unusual or one-off transactions
  • Reviewing customer and revenue concentration
  • Understanding your cash flow and working capital
  • Reviewing debt and other liabilities
  • Testing the assumptions behind financial forecasts
  • Organising supporting documentation

This can help reduce surprises and strengthen confidence in the business.

Financial Due Diligence Is About More Than Numbers

For investors and buyers, financial due diligence provides an opportunity to test whether the business's financial performance, risks and future potential justify the proposed investment or purchase price.

For business owners, it is an opportunity to identify weaknesses before they become negotiation issues.

The objective should be simple:

Know your numbers. Understand your risks. Be prepared to explain both.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is financial due diligence?

Financial due diligence is a detailed review of a company's financial performance, cash flow, assets, liabilities, working capital, forecasts and other financial information before an investment or acquisition.

What do investors look for during financial due diligence?

Investors typically examine revenue quality, profitability, cash flow, working capital, debt, liabilities, customer concentration, financial forecasts and the quality of earnings.

Why is financial due diligence important?

It helps investors and buyers determine whether the company's reported financial performance is accurate, sustainable and consistent with the value being proposed.

What documents are needed for financial due diligence?

Common documents include financial statements, management accounts, bank statements, tax records, customer and revenue information, debt schedules, working capital information and financial forecasts.

What is quality of earnings?

Quality of earnings refers to how well reported profits reflect the underlying and sustainable operating performance of the business, excluding unusual or non-recurring items where appropriate.

Can financial due diligence reduce the value of a business?

Yes. Issues such as weak cash flow, customer concentration, unrecorded liabilities, overstated earnings or unrealistic forecasts can affect an investor's or buyer's assessment of value.
