A contract may look straightforward on the surface, but the real risks often sit in the details. A payment obligation, liability clause, termination provision, or vague scope of work can have a significant impact on a business if it is not properly understood before signing.

For business owners, contract review is not simply about checking legal wording. It is about understanding what the agreement requires, identifying potential exposure, and determining whether the contract supports the commercial objective.

What Is Contract Review?

Contract review is the process of examining an agreement to understand the parties' rights, obligations, risks, and responsibilities before signing or renewing it.

A proper review should consider both the legal implications and the commercial consequences of the agreement.

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate every risk. Rather, it is to ensure that the business understands the risks it is accepting and is comfortable with them.

What Should Business Owners Look for When Reviewing a Contract?

1. Are the Parties and Their Obligations Clear?

Start with the basics.

The contract should accurately identify the parties and clearly establish what each party is expected to provide.

Pay particular attention to:

  • Scope of services or products
  • Deliverables
  • Responsibilities
  • Performance requirements
  • Timelines and milestones

Vague obligations can create different interpretations and become a source of dispute later.

The commercial agreement should be reflected accurately in the written contract.

2. Are the Payment Terms Appropriate?

The headline price is only one part of the financial commitment.

Review:

  • Payment amounts
  • Payment dates
  • Invoicing requirements
  • Payment milestones
  • Taxes and additional charges
  • Late-payment provisions
  • Conditions for withholding or disputing payment

Business owners should also consider the cash-flow impact of the agreed payment structure.

A contract can be profitable on paper while creating unnecessary working-capital pressure.

3. How Can the Contract Be Terminated?

A business should understand how it can exit the agreement before signing it.

Look at:

  • Contract duration
  • Renewal provisions
  • Notice periods
  • Termination for breach
  • Termination for convenience
  • Cure periods
  • Consequences of termination

A particularly important question is:

Can you terminate the contract when you need to, and at what cost?

A contract that provides flexibility to one party but not the other may create an imbalance that should be addressed during negotiation.

4. What Happens If Something Goes Wrong?

This is where liability and indemnity provisions become important.

Businesses should understand:

  • Whether liability is capped
  • The amount of any liability cap
  • What losses are excluded
  • What indemnities are being provided
  • Whether third-party claims are covered
  • Whether there are exceptions to the liability limitations

These provisions can expose a business to obligations that are significantly greater than the original contract value.

Never assume a liability clause is standard simply because it appears in a template.

5. Who Owns the Intellectual Property?

Where a contract involves creative work, technology, consulting, software, designs, content, research, or other intellectual property, ownership should be clear.

The contract should distinguish between:

Existing intellectual property — what each party already owns.

New intellectual property — what is created during the engagement.

It should also establish whether rights are:

  • Transferred
  • Licensed
  • Restricted
  • Shared

This is an area where seemingly simple wording can have significant commercial consequences.

6. Is Confidential Information Properly Protected?

Businesses regularly exchange sensitive information with customers, suppliers, consultants, partners and other third parties.

Confidentiality provisions should address what information is protected, how it may be used, who may access it, and how long the obligation continues.

For businesses handling customer data, proprietary information, pricing, financial information or business strategies, this deserves particular attention.

7. What Law Applies and How Are Disputes Resolved?

The contract should make clear:

  • Governing law
  • Jurisdiction
  • Court or arbitration arrangements
  • Mediation requirements
  • Dispute escalation procedures
  • Relevant location or seat of arbitration where applicable

This becomes particularly important in cross-border contracts, where the parties may operate in different jurisdictions.

Knowing how a dispute will be handled before signing is considerably better than discovering the procedure after a dispute has already arisen.

8. Can the Contract Be Transferred?

Business owners should check whether the agreement can be assigned to another party.

This can become particularly important if the company:

  • Is sold
  • Changes ownership
  • Restructures
  • Acquires another business
  • Transfers activities to another group company

Some contracts require the counterparty's consent before assignment or contain specific change-of-control provisions.

This is particularly relevant when preparing a company for investment or sale.

Contract Review: Legal Risk vs. Commercial Risk

One of the most important points business owners should understand is that legal risk and commercial risk are not always the same thing.

A clause may be legally valid but commercially unfavorable.

For example, a contract may legally allow a customer to terminate on short notice. The legal issue may be straightforward, but the commercial consequence could be significant if that customer represents a large percentage of the company's revenue.

Similarly, a long payment period may be perfectly enforceable but create serious cash-flow pressure.

Good contract review therefore looks beyond whether a clause is legally acceptable and asks whether it makes commercial sense for the business.

Common Contract Review Mistakes

Business owners commonly make the following mistakes:

Reviewing only the price

The commercial price may receive significant attention while liability, termination, renewal and indemnity provisions are overlooked.

Using a template without adapting it

A standard contract may not reflect the specific transaction, business model or risk profile.

Focusing only on the present

A contract should also be considered in the context of future events such as expansion, restructuring, investment, or a potential sale.

Assuming everything is negotiable later

Once a contract is signed, negotiating changes can become considerably more difficult.

Signing without understanding the consequences

The most important question is not whether you have read the contract.

It is whether you understand what you are agreeing to.

When Should a Business Seek Professional Contract Review?

Professional contract review becomes particularly valuable when an agreement:

  • Involves significant financial commitments
  • Creates long-term obligations
  • Contains substantial liability or indemnities
  • Involves intellectual property
  • Relates to an investment, acquisition or partnership
  • Involves parties in different jurisdictions
  • Contains complex termination provisions
  • Could materially affect the business if breached

For routine, low-risk agreements, businesses may have established internal review processes. For material or complex contracts, professional review can help identify risks that may not be obvious from a commercial reading alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I look for when reviewing a contract?

Focus on the scope and obligations, payment terms, termination, liability, indemnities, confidentiality, intellectual property, governing law, dispute resolution, and assignment provisions.

Why is contract review important?

Contract review helps businesses identify unclear obligations, unfavorable provisions, and potential legal or commercial risks before they become disputes or financial problems.

Should a business have every contract reviewed by a lawyer?

Not necessarily. The level of review should reflect the value, complexity and risk of the agreement. Material or complex contracts generally warrant professional legal review.

What is the difference between legal and commercial contract review?

Legal review focuses on rights, obligations, enforceability and legal exposure. Commercial review considers whether the agreement supports the company's financial, operational and strategic objectives. For important contracts, both perspectives should be considered.

Can a contract be negotiated after it has been signed?

Changes may be possible if the parties agree, but it is generally preferable to identify and negotiate important issues before signing.
