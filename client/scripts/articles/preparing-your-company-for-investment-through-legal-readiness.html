Attracting investment is not only about having a strong business model, growing revenue, or presenting an attractive valuation.

Before committing capital, investors will want to understand whether the business is legally structured, properly documented, and ready for scrutiny.

A company that is commercially attractive but legally unprepared can face delays, additional negotiations, or reduced investor confidence.

Legal readiness means putting the company's legal affairs in order before an investor asks to examine them.

What Is Legal Readiness for Investment?

Legal readiness is the process of ensuring that a company has its key legal, corporate and contractual matters properly organised before entering an investment or fundraising process.

It can include reviewing:

  • Corporate structure and ownership
  • Shareholder arrangements
  • Commercial contracts
  • Intellectual property
  • Employment documentation
  • Licences and regulatory compliance
  • Litigation and disputes
  • Material liabilities
  • Corporate records
  • Investment and transaction documents

The objective is simple:

Make the business easier to understand, easier to diligence and easier to invest in.

Why Does Legal Readiness Matter Before Raising Investment?

1. Investors Will Examine the Legal Structure

Investors need to understand what they are investing in and what rights they will receive.

This makes the company's ownership and corporate structure particularly important.

Potential issues may include:

  • Unclear share ownership
  • Informal shareholder arrangements
  • Undocumented transfers
  • Incomplete corporate records
  • Restrictions on issuing or transferring shares

These issues can become significant during investment negotiations.

2. Clean Up Shareholder Arrangements

Shareholder relationships should be clearly documented before bringing in a new investor.

Depending on the circumstances, this may include reviewing:

  • Shareholder agreements
  • Voting rights
  • Management rights
  • Transfer restrictions
  • Pre-emption rights
  • Dividend arrangements
  • Exit provisions

A new investment can change the balance between existing shareholders, making these provisions particularly important.

3. Review Your Key Commercial Contracts

Investors may want to understand how secure the company's revenue and commercial relationships are.

Important contracts may include:

  • Major customer agreements
  • Supplier contracts
  • Distribution agreements
  • Strategic partnerships
  • Technology agreements
  • Long-term commitments

Pay particular attention to termination, assignment and change-of-control provisions.

4. Make Sure Intellectual Property Is Properly Owned

For many businesses, intellectual property is one of their most valuable assets.

Before approaching investors, confirm that the company has appropriate rights to its:

  • Brand
  • Trademarks
  • Software
  • Website and digital assets
  • Content
  • Proprietary processes
  • Technology

This is particularly important where intellectual property has been developed by founders, employees, freelancers or external contractors.

If the company relies on an asset, it should be clear who legally owns it and what rights the company has to use it.

5. Address Outstanding Legal Issues

Investors do not necessarily expect a company to have no legal issues.

However, unresolved disputes, regulatory concerns or contractual problems can create unnecessary uncertainty.

Before fundraising, consider whether there are:

  • Existing disputes
  • Threatened claims
  • Regulatory issues
  • Outstanding obligations
  • Contractual breaches
  • Employee claims

Identifying these matters early gives the company an opportunity to address them before investor due diligence begins.

6. Review Licences and Regulatory Compliance

The company should be able to demonstrate that it is legally authorised to conduct its business.

Depending on the business, this may involve reviewing:

  • Trade licences
  • Regulatory approvals
  • Sector-specific permits
  • Corporate registrations
  • Compliance obligations

For businesses operating in regulated sectors, this can become a major part of investor due diligence.

7. Organise Your Corporate Records

A company may be legally compliant but still appear unprepared if its records are incomplete or difficult to locate.

A well-organised legal data room can make the investment process more efficient.

Relevant documents may include:

  • Corporate documents
  • Shareholder records
  • Material contracts
  • Licences
  • Employment agreements
  • IP documentation
  • Litigation records
  • Previous investment documents

Good organisation can improve investor confidence and reduce unnecessary delays.

Legal Readiness Can Strengthen Your Negotiating Position

Legal preparation is not only about passing investor due diligence.

It can also help founders understand what they are negotiating.

Before accepting investment, founders should understand issues such as:

  • Investor rights
  • Voting rights
  • Board representation
  • Reserved matters
  • Share transfer restrictions
  • Dilution
  • Exit provisions
  • Founder obligations

The strongest position is usually achieved when founders understand these matters before negotiations begin.

What If You Find Problems Before Approaching Investors?

Finding legal issues before fundraising is generally an opportunity—not necessarily a problem.

Depending on the issue, you may be able to:

  • Correct the documentation
  • Resolve outstanding disputes
  • Update corporate records
  • Clarify ownership
  • Renegotiate contracts
  • Obtain missing approvals
  • Formalise shareholder arrangements

Addressing issues early can be significantly easier than discovering them after an investor has already raised concerns.

A Simple Legal Readiness Test

Before approaching investors, ask:

Ownership

Is it clear who owns the company?

Contracts

Are the company's important commercial relationships properly documented?

Intellectual Property

Does the company own or have the necessary rights to its key IP?

Compliance

Are licences, registrations and regulatory requirements up to date?

Disputes

Are there unresolved legal issues that an investor could discover during due diligence?

Corporate Records

Are the company's legal and shareholder records complete and organised?

Investment Structure

Do the founders understand the legal implications of bringing in an investor?

If the answer to any of these is "not sure," it may be worth addressing the issue before entering the investment process.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does legal readiness mean for a company seeking investment?

Legal readiness means ensuring that the company's ownership, corporate records, contracts, intellectual property, licences, compliance and legal obligations are properly organised before investors conduct due diligence.

Why should a company prepare legally before seeking investment?

Early preparation helps identify and resolve legal issues before they become investor concerns. It can also reduce delays and strengthen the company's position during investment negotiations.

What will investors check during legal due diligence?

Investors may review corporate ownership, shareholder arrangements, contracts, intellectual property, licences, employment matters, litigation, liabilities and other material legal documents.

Can legal issues affect a company's valuation?

Yes. Significant legal risks can affect investor confidence, transaction terms and potentially the valuation or structure of an investment.

Should startups also conduct legal readiness reviews?

Yes. Even early-stage companies can benefit from reviewing ownership, founder arrangements, IP ownership, contracts and corporate records before seeking external investment.

What is the difference between legal readiness and legal due diligence?

Legal readiness is preparation by the company before an investment. Legal due diligence is the investor's review of the company as part of evaluating the investment.
