Acquiring a business is a significant commercial decision. Financial performance, growth potential and market position may make a company attractive, but the legal position of the business can materially affect what a buyer is actually acquiring.

This is why legal due diligence should take place before an acquisition is finalised.

Legal due diligence helps buyers identify material legal risks, understand existing obligations and determine whether the transaction terms adequately protect their interests.

The objective is not to eliminate every risk. It is to ensure that important risks are identified before the buyer commits.

What Is Legal Due Diligence in an Acquisition?

Legal due diligence is a structured review of the target company's legal affairs before an acquisition or investment.

Depending on the transaction, it may cover:

  • Corporate ownership and structure
  • Commercial contracts
  • Licences and regulatory compliance
  • Litigation and disputes
  • Employment matters
  • Intellectual property
  • Property and leases
  • Financing and security
  • Liabilities and obligations
  • Related-party arrangements

The scope should reflect the size, nature, industry and structure of the transaction.

Why Does Legal Due Diligence Matter?

1. It Reveals Risks That May Not Appear in the Financials

Financial statements provide important information about a business, but they do not necessarily reveal every legal exposure.

A company may have strong revenue and profitability while also having:

  • Unfavourable long-term contracts
  • Pending disputes
  • Regulatory issues
  • Ownership restrictions
  • Significant contractual liabilities

Legal due diligence provides another layer of visibility before the acquisition.

2. It Confirms What the Buyer Is Actually Acquiring

A buyer needs to understand what assets, rights, contracts and relationships form part of the business.

For example, the buyer may assume that the company owns its:

  • Brand
  • Software
  • Customer contracts
  • Technology
  • Intellectual property

But legal review may reveal that some assets are licensed, jointly owned or subject to restrictions.

This distinction can materially affect the value of the transaction.

3. It Identifies Contractual Risks

Commercial contracts can be among the most valuable—and most overlooked—assets of a business.

Legal due diligence can identify provisions relating to:

  • Change of control
  • Assignment
  • Termination
  • Exclusivity
  • Renewal
  • Liability

A key contract that can be terminated following an acquisition may create a significant commercial risk.

4. It Helps Identify Potential Liabilities

A buyer needs to understand which obligations could remain with or affect the business after completion.

These may include:

  • Guarantees
  • Indemnities
  • Litigation exposure
  • Regulatory obligations
  • Long-term commitments
  • Employment liabilities
  • Related-party arrangements

The objective is to avoid discovering material obligations after the transaction has already closed.

5. It Supports Better Negotiation

Due diligence findings can strengthen the buyer's negotiating position.

Depending on the issue, the buyer may negotiate:

  • Purchase price adjustments
  • Seller warranties
  • Specific indemnities
  • Conditions before completion
  • Escrow arrangements
  • Remediation by the seller
  • Changes to the transaction structure

In this sense, legal due diligence is not only about risk identification.

It can directly influence the terms of the deal.

6. It Helps Buyers Decide Whether to Proceed

Not every legal issue is a reason to walk away.

The important question is whether the risk is:

Material → Manageable → Acceptable

A relatively minor contractual issue may require no more than clarification.

A significant dispute, ownership problem or regulatory issue may require resolution before completion.

In some cases, the findings may justify reconsidering the transaction altogether.

7. It Protects the Buyer After Completion

Some risks cannot realistically be resolved before closing.

Where appropriate, the buyer may seek contractual protection through:

  • Representations and warranties
  • Specific indemnities
  • Liability limitations
  • Escrow or retention arrangements
  • Conditions precedent

The objective is to ensure that the transaction documents reflect the risks identified during due diligence.

What Happens If Due Diligence Is Skipped?

Skipping or limiting legal due diligence can create significant problems.

A buyer may discover after completion that:

  • A major contract can be terminated
  • A regulatory approval is missing
  • An important asset is not owned by the company
  • Litigation is already underway
  • Employees have outstanding claims
  • The company has undisclosed obligations

At that stage, the buyer's ability to negotiate may be severely limited.

The earlier a material risk is identified, the more options the buyer usually has to address it.

Legal Due Diligence Is Not About Finding a "Perfect" Business

Every business carries some level of legal risk.

The purpose of due diligence is not to find a company with zero risk.

It is to understand:

What are the risks?

How significant are they?

Who should bear them?

Can they be managed or protected against?

Do they change the value or structure of the transaction?

This is what turns due diligence from a compliance exercise into a commercial decision-making process.

When Should Legal Due Diligence Begin?

Legal due diligence should ideally begin early in the acquisition process, once sufficient information about the target is available.

Starting early gives the buyer time to:

  • Request additional documentation
  • Investigate concerns
  • Engage specialist advisors
  • Negotiate protections
  • Resolve material issues
  • Reconsider the transaction if necessary

Waiting until the final stages can leave insufficient time to respond effectively to significant findings.

Legal Due Diligence and Financial Due Diligence Work Together

The strongest acquisition process considers both legal and financial perspectives.

Financial due diligence asks questions such as:

  • Is the business financially healthy?
  • Are revenues sustainable?
  • Are margins reliable?
  • What is the company's financial position?

Legal due diligence asks:

  • What obligations and liabilities exist?
  • Are key contracts secure?
  • Is the business properly licensed?
  • Who owns the assets and IP?
  • Are there disputes or legal exposures?

Together, they provide a more complete picture of the acquisition opportunity.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is legal due diligence important before an acquisition?

It helps buyers identify legal risks, obligations, ownership issues, contractual restrictions, disputes and compliance concerns before completing the transaction.

What happens if legal due diligence finds a problem?

The buyer may request further investigation, require the seller to resolve the issue, negotiate a price adjustment, seek warranties or indemnities, restructure the transaction, or decide not to proceed.

Can legal due diligence affect the purchase price?

Yes. Material legal risks can affect the perceived value of the business and may lead to price adjustments or additional contractual protections.

Is legal due diligence required for every acquisition?

The scope will vary depending on the size and nature of the transaction. However, legal due diligence is particularly important where the acquisition involves significant investment, complex contracts, regulated activities or material legal exposure.

When should legal due diligence start?

It should generally start early enough to allow the buyer to investigate issues and negotiate appropriate protections before the transaction becomes irreversible.

Is legal due diligence the same as financial due diligence?

No. Financial due diligence focuses on the financial position and performance of the business, while legal due diligence focuses on its legal rights, obligations, contracts, compliance and potential legal exposure.
