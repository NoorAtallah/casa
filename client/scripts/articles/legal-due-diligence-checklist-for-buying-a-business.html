Buying a business involves more than reviewing its financial statements. A company may have strong revenues and growth potential while carrying legal, contractual, regulatory or ownership risks that could affect the transaction.

A legal due diligence checklist helps buyers identify the areas that should be reviewed before committing to an acquisition.

The purpose is not simply to collect documents. It is to understand what you are acquiring, what obligations come with it, and which risks may require protection or negotiation before completion.

What Should Be Included in a Legal Due Diligence Checklist?

The exact scope depends on the size, industry and structure of the transaction. However, buyers should generally consider the following areas.

1. Corporate Structure & Ownership

Confirm the legal identity and ownership of the target business.

Review:

  • Certificate of incorporation and constitutional documents
  • Current shareholding structure
  • Shareholder agreements
  • Corporate registers
  • Board and shareholder resolutions
  • Existing ownership restrictions
  • Authority to sell the shares or assets

Key question: Does the seller have the legal authority to transfer what you are acquiring?

2. Licences & Regulatory Compliance

Establish whether the business is properly authorised to conduct its activities.

Review:

  • Trade and commercial licences
  • Regulatory approvals
  • Permits
  • Sector-specific registrations
  • Material compliance requirements
  • Regulatory notices or violations

For regulated businesses, this area may require more extensive specialist review.

3. Material Commercial Contracts

Identify the contracts that are essential to the business.

Review:

  • Major customer agreements
  • Supplier contracts
  • Distribution agreements
  • Partnership agreements
  • Service agreements
  • Technology agreements
  • Financing arrangements

Pay particular attention to:

Change-of-control | Assignment | Termination | Renewal | Exclusivity | Liability

A buyer should understand whether an acquisition could affect the continuation of key contracts.

4. Litigation & Disputes

Determine whether the business is involved in existing or potential disputes.

Check for:

  • Court proceedings
  • Arbitration
  • Regulatory investigations
  • Customer or supplier disputes
  • Employment claims
  • Settlement agreements
  • Threatened claims

The review should consider not only current disputes but also circumstances that could result in future claims.

5. Employment & Management

Review the legal relationship between the business and its employees and key management.

Consider:

  • Employment contracts
  • Senior management agreements
  • Employee claims
  • Outstanding entitlements
  • Restrictive covenants
  • Termination obligations
  • Confidentiality arrangements

Key personnel may be particularly important where the value of the business depends heavily on their knowledge or relationships.

6. Intellectual Property

Identify the intellectual property that contributes to the value of the business.

Review:

  • Trademarks
  • Copyright
  • Patents
  • Software
  • Domain names
  • Proprietary content
  • IP licences
  • Third-party rights

Key question: Does the business actually own—or have the right to use—the IP that the buyer believes it is acquiring?

7. Property, Leases & Key Assets

Review the legal status of important physical assets and business premises.

Consider:

  • Property ownership
  • Commercial leases
  • Equipment agreements
  • Asset ownership
  • Security interests
  • Transfer restrictions
  • Landlord consent requirements

This can be particularly important where the location or physical assets are essential to continued operations.

8. Liabilities & Obligations

Look beyond the company's headline financial position.

Identify potential:

  • Guarantees
  • Indemnities
  • Security arrangements
  • Long-term commitments
  • Unusual contractual obligations
  • Related-party obligations
  • Potential claims

The objective is to understand what obligations may remain with or affect the business after completion.

9. Financing & Security

Review existing financing arrangements and whether assets or shares have been pledged or otherwise restricted.

Consider:

  • Loan agreements
  • Security documents
  • Guarantees
  • Charges
  • Restrictions on ownership changes
  • Consent requirements

Existing financing arrangements may directly affect the structure or timing of an acquisition.

10. Related-Party Transactions

Identify transactions between the target and its shareholders, directors, owners or related entities.

Examples include:

  • Shareholder loans
  • Related-party service agreements
  • Shared resources
  • Intercompany arrangements
  • Related-party leases

These arrangements may need to be terminated, transferred or renegotiated as part of the acquisition.

11. Tax & Regulatory Exposure

Legal due diligence should work alongside tax and financial due diligence.

Consider whether there are:

  • Outstanding tax obligations
  • Regulatory penalties
  • Unresolved government claims
  • Historical compliance issues
  • Disputed assessments

Specialist tax advice should be obtained where appropriate.

12. Transaction Documents & Seller Protections

Finally, the findings from due diligence should feed directly into the acquisition documents.

Depending on the transaction, buyers may require:

  • Seller warranties
  • Specific indemnities
  • Conditions precedent
  • Price adjustments
  • Escrow or retention arrangements
  • Remediation before completion

Due diligence is most valuable when its findings are used to protect the buyer.

A Practical Way to Use the Checklist

A checklist should not become a box-ticking exercise.

For every material issue identified, the buyer should consider three questions:

What is the risk?

Understand the legal or contractual issue.

What could it mean financially or commercially?

Assess its potential impact on the business and transaction.

What should we do about it?

Determine whether the issue should be resolved, negotiated, protected against or accepted.

This approach turns legal due diligence into a decision-making tool rather than simply a document review.

When Should Buyers Start Legal Due Diligence?

Ideally, legal due diligence should begin as early as practical in the acquisition process.

Starting early gives buyers sufficient time to:

  • Request missing information
  • Investigate potential risks
  • Obtain specialist advice
  • Negotiate transaction protections
  • Resolve issues before completion

Discovering a major legal issue immediately before signing can significantly reduce the buyer's negotiating position.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a legal due diligence checklist?

A legal due diligence checklist is a structured framework used to identify and review the key legal, contractual, regulatory, ownership and liability matters associated with a business acquisition.

What documents should a buyer request during legal due diligence?

Documents commonly include corporate records, licences, material contracts, employment agreements, litigation records, intellectual property documents, leases, financing agreements and information relating to liabilities and related-party transactions.

Is legal due diligence necessary when buying a small business?

The scope may be smaller, but legal due diligence can still be valuable. Even a small business can have contractual, employment, regulatory, ownership or liability risks that affect the acquisition.

Can legal due diligence change the purchase price?

Yes. Material legal risks may lead to a price adjustment, additional warranties, indemnities, escrow arrangements or other transaction protections.

Who conducts legal due diligence?

Legal due diligence is generally conducted by qualified legal advisors or lawyers, often working alongside financial, tax and other specialist advisors depending on the nature of the transaction.

Is a checklist enough to complete legal due diligence?

No. A checklist helps structure the review, but effective due diligence requires professional assessment of the documents, identification of material risks and evaluation of their impact on the transaction.
