Selling a business is not simply about finding a buyer and agreeing on a price.

Before entering the market, business owners should understand what their business is worth, what supports that value and what could reduce it.

A well-prepared valuation can help owners set realistic expectations, identify weaknesses before buyers do, and strengthen their position during negotiations.

The important point is simple:

The best time to prepare for a business valuation is before you decide to sell.

Why Is Business Valuation Important Before a Sale?

A valuation provides a structured view of the business's potential market value based on its financial performance, assets, cash flow, growth prospects, risks and market position.

Without preparation, owners may:

  • Overestimate the value of their business
  • Set an unrealistic asking price
  • Miss issues that buyers may identify
  • Enter negotiations without sufficient evidence
  • Lose leverage during the transaction

Preparation allows owners to address potential concerns before they become negotiation points.

1. Understand What Is Driving Your Business Value

Before selling, identify the factors that make the business attractive.

These may include:

  • Strong and consistent profitability
  • Recurring revenue
  • Long-term customer relationships
  • Strong cash flow
  • Market position
  • Intellectual property
  • Experienced management
  • Growth opportunities
  • Diversified customer base

Understanding these value drivers helps owners communicate the commercial strength of the business, rather than focusing only on historical revenue.

2. Review Your Financial Performance

Buyers will closely examine the financial history of the business.

Before going to market, review:

  • Revenue trends
  • Gross margins
  • EBITDA
  • Operating expenses
  • Net profit
  • Cash flow
  • Working capital

More importantly, be able to explain why the numbers have changed over time.

A clear financial story can make the business easier for a buyer to understand and assess.

3. Identify and Normalise One-Off Items

Business accounts may contain expenses or income that do not reflect normal operations.

Examples may include:

  • One-off legal costs
  • Exceptional expenses
  • Non-recurring income
  • Owner-related expenses
  • Unusual transactions

Identifying these items can help establish a clearer picture of the business's underlying earnings.

4. Clean Up the Balance Sheet

A buyer will want confidence that the balance sheet accurately reflects the business.

Review:

  • Receivables
  • Inventory
  • Fixed assets
  • Cash
  • Loans
  • Payables
  • Accrued expenses
  • Other liabilities

Old, unexplained or unreconciled balances can create unnecessary questions during due diligence.

5. Assess Customer Concentration

If a significant percentage of revenue comes from one customer, a buyer may consider this a risk.

Before selling, understand:

  • Your largest customers
  • Revenue concentration
  • Contract duration
  • Customer retention
  • Renewal rates
  • Dependence on individual relationships

Where possible, diversifying revenue can strengthen the business's risk profile before a sale.

6. Reduce Founder Dependency

A business that relies heavily on its owner can be more difficult to transfer.

Ask:

Could the business continue operating effectively if the owner left?

If the answer is no, consider strengthening:

  • Management structures
  • Delegation
  • Processes
  • Documentation
  • Customer relationship management
  • Succession arrangements

A transferable business can be more attractive to potential buyers.

7. Review Legal and Commercial Risks

Valuation is not purely a financial exercise.

Potential buyers may also examine:

  • Customer contracts
  • Supplier agreements
  • Employment arrangements
  • Intellectual property
  • Licences
  • Regulatory compliance
  • Litigation
  • Outstanding claims
  • Corporate records

Unresolved legal or commercial issues can affect both valuation and transaction terms.

8. Validate Your Growth Projections

Future growth can support a higher valuation—but only when the assumptions are credible.

Review:

  • Revenue growth assumptions
  • New customer acquisition
  • Pricing
  • Market expansion
  • Capacity
  • Staffing requirements
  • Capital expenditure
  • Working capital needs

A buyer is likely to challenge projections that appear overly optimistic or unsupported.

9. Consider the Appropriate Valuation Method

Different businesses may require different valuation approaches.

Common approaches include:

Income-Based Approach

Values the business based on expected future earnings or cash flows.

Market-Based Approach

Uses comparable companies or relevant transactions to assess valuation multiples.

Asset-Based Approach

Considers the value of the company's assets and liabilities.

The appropriate methodology depends on the business, industry, financial profile and transaction circumstances.

10. Prepare Before Buyers Start Asking Questions

One of the biggest advantages of early preparation is the ability to identify issues before they appear in due diligence.

Create a review covering:

Financials → Operations → Customers → Contracts → Assets → Liabilities → Management → Risks → Growth

The objective is not to make the business appear perfect.

It is to ensure that the business is understood, defensible and transaction-ready.

What Can Reduce Business Value Before a Sale?

Common issues include:

  • Declining profitability
  • Weak cash flow
  • Customer concentration
  • Founder dependency
  • Poor financial records
  • Unresolved legal disputes
  • Regulatory exposure
  • Excessive debt
  • Weak contracts
  • Unrealistic projections
  • Undocumented liabilities

Addressing these issues early may help protect the value of the business and reduce surprises during negotiations.

How Early Should You Prepare?

Ideally, well before the business is formally marketed for sale.

The earlier you begin, the more time you have to:

  • Improve profitability
  • Strengthen cash flow
  • Reduce unnecessary costs
  • Diversify customers
  • Resolve legal issues
  • Improve reporting
  • Strengthen management
  • Document processes
  • Build a credible growth story

Business value is usually built over time—not during the final weeks before a transaction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why should I value my business before selling it?

A valuation helps establish a realistic understanding of the business's value and allows you to identify financial, operational or legal issues that could affect negotiations.

When should I start preparing my business for sale?

Ideally, preparation should begin well before the business is marketed, giving you time to improve performance and address potential concerns.

What factors affect the value of a business before a sale?

Key factors include profitability, cash flow, recurring revenue, customer concentration, growth prospects, assets, liabilities, management, market position and risk.

Can improving profitability increase business value?

Yes. Stronger and more sustainable profitability can improve the financial profile of a business and may support a stronger valuation, depending on the applicable valuation methodology.

Does founder dependency affect business valuation?

It can. A business that depends heavily on its owner may be viewed as carrying greater transition risk than a business with established management, documented processes and transferable customer relationships.

Should legal issues be resolved before selling a business?

Where possible, yes. Unresolved legal or regulatory issues may create additional due diligence concerns and potentially affect valuation, transaction terms or the buyer's willingness to proceed.
