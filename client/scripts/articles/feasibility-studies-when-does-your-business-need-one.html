Not every business decision requires a feasibility study. But when a decision involves significant investment, expansion, a new market, a new product or substantial financial risk, relying on assumptions alone can be costly.

A feasibility study provides a structured assessment of whether a proposed business initiative is commercially viable, financially sustainable and operationally achievable before significant resources are committed.

For business owners, the real value is not simply confirming whether an idea can work—it is understanding whether it makes sense to pursue, how it should be structured, and what risks need to be addressed first.

What Is a Feasibility Study?

A feasibility study is an objective assessment of a proposed business project or investment.

It typically examines:

  • Market opportunity
  • Commercial viability
  • Financial requirements
  • Revenue potential
  • Operating costs
  • Operational requirements
  • Legal and regulatory considerations
  • Key risks
  • Expected returns

The outcome should help management decide whether to proceed, modify, delay or reject the proposed initiative.

When Does a Business Need a Feasibility Study?

A feasibility study is particularly valuable when the decision involves significant resources, uncertainty or irreversible commitments.

Here are some common situations.

1. Entering a New Market

Entering a new geographic or customer market can involve significant investment.

A feasibility study can help assess:

  • Market demand
  • Customer segments
  • Competition
  • Pricing
  • Entry barriers
  • Regulatory requirements
  • Expected investment
  • Potential returns

This can help determine whether the opportunity justifies the resources required to enter the market.

2. Expanding an Existing Business

Expansion may involve new premises, employees, equipment, technology and working capital.

Before committing to expansion, a feasibility study can test whether:

Expected additional returns justify the investment and additional risk.

It can also identify operational constraints that may not be obvious from the financial projections alone.

3. Launching a New Product or Service

A new product may appear commercially attractive but still fail to generate sufficient returns.

A feasibility assessment can examine:

  • Customer demand
  • Pricing
  • Development costs
  • Marketing requirements
  • Production or delivery capacity
  • Competition
  • Expected margins

The objective is to establish whether the new offering has a realistic path to profitability.

4. Establishing a New Business

Entrepreneurs often start with a strong idea.

The feasibility study tests that idea against commercial reality.

It may assess:

  • Target market
  • Business model
  • Startup costs
  • Operating expenses
  • Revenue assumptions
  • Break-even point
  • Funding requirements
  • Key risks

This provides a stronger foundation for deciding whether and how to proceed.

5. Making a Significant Investment

A feasibility study can be particularly useful before committing substantial capital to:

  • Property
  • Equipment
  • Technology
  • Infrastructure
  • New facilities
  • Production capacity
  • Strategic projects

The larger the investment, the more important it becomes to understand the assumptions behind the expected return.

6. Considering a Joint Venture or Strategic Partnership

A joint venture may provide access to new markets, technology, customers or expertise.

But the opportunity should be assessed beyond the potential commercial upside.

A feasibility study can consider:

  • Strategic fit
  • Financial contribution
  • Revenue potential
  • Responsibilities
  • Governance
  • Risks
  • Exit considerations

This helps determine whether the proposed partnership is commercially sustainable.

7. Considering an Acquisition

A feasibility assessment can help determine whether acquiring another business makes strategic and financial sense.

It may consider:

  • Purchase price
  • Expected synergies
  • Funding requirements
  • Existing liabilities
  • Revenue potential
  • Integration costs
  • Operational compatibility
  • Expected return

This should complement—not replace—proper legal, financial and commercial due diligence.

Feasibility Study vs. Business Plan

These are related but serve different purposes.

A feasibility study primarily asks:

“Does this opportunity make commercial and financial sense?”

A business plan generally asks:

“How will we execute and grow the business?”

In some situations, the feasibility study should come first. Once the opportunity has been validated, the findings can then be used to develop a more detailed business plan.

What Makes a Feasibility Study Valuable?

The value of a feasibility study depends heavily on the quality of its assumptions.

A useful study should:

  • Use credible market information
  • Clearly identify assumptions
  • Test different scenarios
  • Distinguish facts from estimates
  • Consider downside risks
  • Link financial projections to operational realities
  • Provide an objective recommendation

A feasibility study should challenge the business idea, not simply justify it.

Common Mistakes Businesses Make

Starting with the desired conclusion

If management has already decided to proceed, the study may become a justification exercise rather than an objective assessment.

Overestimating revenue

Projected revenue should be supported by realistic market assumptions and a credible route to customers.

Underestimating costs

Businesses often focus on startup costs while overlooking ongoing operating expenses and working capital requirements.

Ignoring downside scenarios

A project that works only under optimistic assumptions may not be sufficiently robust.

Looking only at financial projections

Financial viability depends on market demand, operations, people, regulation and execution.

Treating the feasibility study as a guarantee

A feasibility study improves decision-making; it does not eliminate business risk or guarantee future performance.

When Is a Feasibility Study Probably Not Necessary?

Not every decision requires a formal feasibility study.

It may be unnecessary where:

  • The investment is relatively small
  • The decision is easily reversible
  • The market is already well understood
  • The business has significant existing experience in the activity
  • The financial exposure is limited

However, even where a full feasibility study is unnecessary, a structured commercial and financial assessment can still be valuable.

A Simple Feasibility Test

Before committing significant resources, ask:

1. Is there sufficient market demand?

2. Can the business compete effectively?

3. What will the project actually cost?

4. How much working capital will be required?

5. What level of revenue is realistically achievable?

6. When could the project reach break-even?

7. What are the major risks?

8. What happens if the assumptions are wrong?

9. Does the opportunity fit the company's strategy?

10. Is the expected return sufficient for the risk being taken?

If these questions cannot be answered with reasonable confidence, further analysis may be warranted before proceeding.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a feasibility study?

A feasibility study is an assessment of whether a proposed business project or investment is commercially viable, financially sustainable and operationally achievable.

When should a business conduct a feasibility study?

It is particularly useful before significant investments, market entry, business expansion, new products or services, major strategic projects, joint ventures or acquisitions.

What does a feasibility study include?

It may assess market demand, competition, business model, financial projections, investment requirements, operations, legal and regulatory considerations, risks and expected returns.

Is a feasibility study the same as a business plan?

No. A feasibility study primarily determines whether an opportunity is viable, while a business plan focuses more on how the business will operate and execute its strategy.

How much does a feasibility study cost?

The cost depends on the size, complexity and scope of the project. A simple internal project requires a very different level of analysis from a major investment or market-entry initiative.

Can a feasibility study guarantee that a business idea will succeed?

No. A feasibility study cannot eliminate uncertainty or guarantee success. Its purpose is to improve the quality of the decision by identifying opportunities, assumptions and risks before resources are committed.
