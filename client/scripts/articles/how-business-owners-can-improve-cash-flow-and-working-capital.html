Strong cash flow gives a business the flexibility to operate, invest and grow.

Even profitable businesses can experience cash pressure when money is tied up in unpaid invoices, inventory, supplier commitments or rapid expansion.

Improving cash flow is therefore not simply about increasing sales. It is about improving how quickly and efficiently the business converts activity into available cash.

How Can a Business Improve Cash Flow and Working Capital?

Business owners can improve liquidity by focusing on receivables, inventory, payables, cash forecasting, pricing, spending and the timing of major investments.

The right priorities depend on the business model, but the following areas are a useful starting point.

1. Improve the Collection of Receivables

One of the fastest ways to improve cash flow may be to improve how quickly customers pay.

Review:

  • Customer payment terms
  • Overdue invoices
  • Credit limits
  • Collection procedures
  • Invoicing accuracy
  • Advance payment or deposit requirements

A business can increase sales while simultaneously increasing its cash flow problem if customers take too long to pay.

Revenue is not cash until it is collected.

2. Reduce Unnecessary Inventory

For businesses that hold stock, excessive inventory can tie up significant amounts of cash.

Review:

  • Slow-moving products
  • Overstocking
  • Purchasing patterns
  • Inventory turnover
  • Minimum stock levels

The objective is not simply to reduce inventory.

It is to maintain the right level of stock without unnecessarily locking cash into products that are not moving.

3. Review Supplier Payment Terms

Supplier relationships can have a direct effect on working capital.

Where commercially appropriate, consider whether payment terms can be better aligned with customer collections.

For example, a business that receives customer payment after 60 days but must pay suppliers within 15 days may face unnecessary working capital pressure.

The objective is to create a healthier cash conversion cycle.

4. Build a Rolling Cash Flow Forecast

A cash flow forecast gives management visibility over expected cash inflows and outflows.

It can help answer:

  • How much cash will we have next month?
  • When will major payments be due?
  • When are customer collections expected?
  • Can we afford planned investments?
  • When could a cash shortage occur?

The value is not simply in predicting the future.

It is in identifying potential problems early enough to act.

5. Understand the Cash Conversion Cycle

The cash conversion cycle helps businesses understand how long cash remains tied up in operations.

It is influenced by:

Receivables → Inventory → Payables → Cash

A business should understand how long it takes to:

  • Purchase or produce its offering
  • Sell it
  • Collect the customer's payment

The shorter and more efficiently managed this cycle is, the less working capital may be required.

6. Review Pricing and Payment Structures

Pricing decisions can also affect cash flow.

Consider whether the business can use:

  • Deposits
  • Milestone payments
  • Retainers
  • Shorter payment terms
  • Recurring billing
  • Upfront payments

This can be particularly useful for professional services, project-based businesses and businesses with significant upfront costs.

7. Identify Unnecessary Cash Outflows

Cash flow improvement does not always require cutting costs across the board.

Instead, review spending and distinguish between:

Essential expenditure → Growth investment → Discretionary expenditure

Look for costs that provide limited commercial value or could be renegotiated, reduced or postponed.

The objective is better allocation of cash, not simply lower spending.

8. Manage Growth Carefully

Growth can consume cash before it generates it.

Before taking on significant new customers, locations or projects, consider:

  • Additional staffing
  • Inventory requirements
  • Supplier payments
  • Marketing expenditure
  • Equipment
  • Working capital requirements

A growth plan should therefore include a cash flow impact assessment, not only a revenue forecast.

9. Separate Profitability From Liquidity

Business owners should monitor both.

A company can have:

Strong revenue + strong profit + weak cash flow

or:

Moderate profit + strong cash generation

The objective is to understand why.

Key metrics may include:

  • Gross margin
  • EBITDA
  • Operating cash flow
  • Accounts receivable
  • Days sales outstanding
  • Inventory turnover
  • Accounts payable
  • Working capital
  • Cash conversion

10. Set a Minimum Cash Requirement

Businesses should consider establishing an appropriate minimum cash reserve based on their operating model and obligations.

The required level will vary depending on:

  • Monthly operating costs
  • Revenue stability
  • Customer payment cycles
  • Debt obligations
  • Seasonality
  • Business growth plans

The purpose is to avoid making major decisions from a position of immediate liquidity pressure.

What Is the Best Way to Improve Working Capital?

There is no single solution.

A business with slow-paying customers may need to focus on receivables.

A retailer with excessive stock may need to focus on inventory.

A rapidly growing company may need better cash forecasting and funding.

A project-based business may need to rethink payment structures.

The important first step is to identify where cash is getting trapped.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can a business improve cash flow quickly?

Businesses can often start by accelerating customer collections, reviewing overdue receivables, controlling unnecessary inventory, managing supplier terms and postponing non-essential cash expenditure.

What is working capital?

Working capital generally reflects the resources tied up in a business's day-to-day operations, particularly receivables, inventory and payables.

How does working capital affect cash flow?

Poor working capital management can leave cash tied up in receivables or inventory, while effective management can improve the timing and availability of cash.

What is the cash conversion cycle?

The cash conversion cycle measures the time it takes for a business to convert cash invested in operations back into cash collected from customers, taking inventory, receivables and payables into account.

Does increasing sales always improve cash flow?

No. Rapid sales growth can actually create cash pressure if the business needs to spend cash on inventory, employees or suppliers before collecting payment from customers.

How often should a business review working capital?

The appropriate frequency depends on the business. Companies with rapid growth, long collection cycles or tight liquidity may benefit from regular, preferably monthly, working capital reviews.
