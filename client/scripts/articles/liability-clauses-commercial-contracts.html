Liability clauses are among the most commercially important provisions in a contract because they determine who bears the financial consequences when something goes wrong.

A contract may clearly define the services, price and responsibilities, but if liability is not properly addressed, a business could face exposure far beyond the value of the original transaction.

For business owners, the key question is not simply “Who is responsible?” but rather “What is the extent of that responsibility, and is the risk proportionate to the commercial relationship?”

What Is a Liability Clause?

A liability clause sets out the circumstances in which a party may be responsible for loss, damage, costs or claims arising from the contract.

Depending on the agreement and applicable law, liability provisions may address:

  • Direct and indirect losses
  • Financial caps
  • Exclusions
  • Indemnities
  • Third-party claims
  • Breach of contract
  • Negligence or misconduct
  • Confidentiality and data breaches
  • Intellectual property infringement

The exact effect of a liability clause depends on its wording and the applicable legal framework.

1. Liability Caps

A liability cap limits the maximum amount one party may be required to pay for certain claims.

For example, liability might be capped at:

  • The total contract value
  • Fees paid during a specific period
  • A fixed monetary amount
  • A multiple of the contract value

A cap can provide valuable certainty, but businesses should consider whether the proposed limit is commercially reasonable given the potential exposure.

2. Excluded or Indirect Losses

Contracts often attempt to exclude certain categories of loss, such as:

  • Loss of profit
  • Loss of revenue
  • Loss of business
  • Loss of opportunity
  • Consequential or indirect losses

However, the meaning and enforceability of these exclusions can vary depending on the wording and applicable law.

Businesses should therefore understand exactly what they may be giving up by accepting an exclusion.

3. Exceptions to the Liability Cap

A liability cap is rarely effective in isolation.

Some contracts provide that certain liabilities are not subject to the agreed cap.

These may include matters such as:

  • Fraud
  • Wilful misconduct
  • Confidentiality breaches
  • Intellectual property infringement
  • Certain indemnity obligations
  • Personal injury or death, where applicable

The exceptions can sometimes create greater exposure than the general liability cap itself.

4. Indemnities

An indemnity can create a separate and potentially significant financial obligation.

Under an indemnity, one party may agree to compensate the other for specified losses, claims or liabilities.

Business owners should understand:

  • What triggers the indemnity
  • What losses are covered
  • Whether there is a financial cap
  • Whether the indemnity sits inside or outside the liability cap
  • What procedures apply to third-party claims

An indemnity should not be accepted simply because it appears as standard contractual wording.

5. Third-Party Claims

A business may face claims from customers, employees, regulators or other third parties because of the actions of its contractual counterparty.

The contract should therefore clearly establish, where appropriate:

  • Who handles the claim
  • Who bears the cost
  • Who controls the defence
  • Whether consent is required before settlement
  • Whether indemnification applies

This can become particularly important in outsourcing, technology, construction and professional services arrangements.

6. Intellectual Property Liability

Contracts involving software, branding, designs, content or technology may expose businesses to intellectual property claims.

The agreement should clarify who is responsible if a third party alleges that:

  • Software infringes its rights
  • Content was used without permission
  • A trademark was improperly used
  • Technology infringes intellectual property rights

The allocation of this risk should reflect who controls the relevant asset and who is in the best position to manage the risk.

7. Confidentiality and Data-Related Liability

A breach of confidential information or personal data can have significant commercial and regulatory consequences.

Businesses should consider whether liability provisions adequately address:

  • Unauthorised disclosure
  • Data breaches
  • Cyber incidents
  • Loss of confidential information
  • Regulatory penalties or claims, where legally recoverable

These risks deserve particular attention where a supplier or service provider has access to sensitive information.

8. Liability for Subcontractors

A company may use subcontractors while remaining contractually responsible to its customer.

The contract should clarify whether a party is responsible for:

  • Its employees
  • Agents
  • Consultants
  • Subcontractors
  • Other third parties it engages

This can prevent uncertainty over who bears responsibility when work is delegated.

9. Insurance and Liability

Insurance and contractual liability should be considered together.

Businesses should assess whether their insurance coverage is consistent with the liabilities they are accepting under the contract.

For example, a business may agree to a significant contractual liability while carrying insurance that provides inadequate protection.

The contract should not create obligations that the business has not realistically considered or insured against.

10. Unlimited Liability

Some contracts contain provisions under which certain liabilities are unlimited or are excluded from the general liability cap.

This can materially change the risk profile of the agreement.

Before accepting such provisions, businesses should understand:

  • Which liabilities are unlimited
  • Why they are treated differently
  • Whether the exposure is commercially acceptable
  • Whether insurance is available
  • Whether a negotiated cap is appropriate

A Practical Liability Review

Before signing a commercial contract, business owners should ask:

Common Liability Clause Mistakes

Accepting a liability cap without checking the exceptions

A seemingly reasonable cap may become much less protective if important liabilities are excluded from it.

Treating indemnities as standard wording

An indemnity can create substantial financial exposure and should be understood before acceptance.

Ignoring insurance

Contractual liability and insurance coverage should be assessed together.

Accepting broad exclusions

A broad exclusion of loss may significantly restrict your ability to recover damages.

Failing to consider third-party claims

Businesses should understand who bears the cost if a customer's or another third party's claim arises.

Why Liability Clauses Matter to Business Owners

Liability provisions are fundamentally about risk allocation.

Two contracts may have exactly the same commercial value but create very different levels of risk depending on how liability is structured.

A business should therefore assess not only:

“How much will we earn from this contract?”

but also:

“What is the maximum exposure we are accepting in exchange for that revenue?”

That question can be particularly important for high-value, long-term, technology, professional services and cross-border contracts.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a liability clause in a commercial contract?

A liability clause establishes when a party may be responsible for losses or claims and the extent of that responsibility.

What is a liability cap?

A liability cap limits the amount a party may be required to pay for specified contractual claims or losses, subject to any agreed exceptions.

What is an indemnity?

An indemnity is a contractual obligation under which one party agrees to compensate another for specified losses, claims or liabilities, subject to the terms of the agreement and applicable law.

Should liability always be capped?

Not necessarily. The appropriate approach depends on the nature of the contract, the risks involved, bargaining position and applicable law. Certain liabilities may also be treated differently under the agreement or applicable legislation.

What is the difference between a liability cap and an exclusion?

A liability cap limits the amount of exposure, while an exclusion seeks to remove certain types of loss or liability from the scope of recovery altogether.

Why should businesses review liability clauses before signing?

Because liability provisions determine how much financial risk a business may assume if something goes wrong. A clause that appears standard can create significant exposure depending on its wording and exceptions.
